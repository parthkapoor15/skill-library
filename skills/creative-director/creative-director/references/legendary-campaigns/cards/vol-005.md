# Get a Mac (Mac vs. PC) (Apple · 2006)

## Insight
The PC vs. Mac divide was about more than computing — it was an identity statement. PC users were workers, conformists, people who did what the corporate world demanded. Mac users were creatives, individuals, people who chose differently. Apple's insight: make the product choice a character choice, and personify both characters so the audience knows immediately which one they want to be.

## Mechanic
A long-running campaign of 66 spots over four years, each featuring "Mac" (Justin Long — young, casual, creative) and "PC" (John Hodgman — older, awkward, corporate) in dialogue. The conflict (P02) was always between two clearly differentiated characters. P11 operated through Hodgman's increasingly absurd situations and rationalizations. Each spot demonstrated a specific product advantage through the characters' interaction rather than through specification. Never a screenshot. Never a demo. Pure character.

## Why it worked
Enemy-based positioning (P02) at its most refined: the opponent was lovable enough to watch but clearly wrong. Hodgman's PC was not hateful — he was recognizably, painfully corporate, which meant Mac's casual ease was the obviously preferable alternative. The campaign ran 66 spots without a single weak execution because the brief was infinitely flexible: any situation where Mac is better than PC generated a new spot. The characters were so precisely calibrated that audiences identified immediately. The tagline "Get a Mac" was the call to action after the characters had already done the work.

## Steal
When your competitor is genuinely inferior at something, personify both brands as characters and let the audience observe the difference rather than claim it. Make the competitor sympathetic enough to be watchable, and the contrast becomes the argument.

# HomePod: Welcome Home (Apple · 2018)

## Insight

After a draining day in the world, home should transform into a space that belongs entirely to you — not just a place you return to, but one that expands with your emotional state.

## Mechanic

Spike Jonze-directed five-minute film: a woman comes home exhausted, plays music on HomePod, and the apartment transforms into a vast surrealist landscape that mirrors her emotional journey from fatigue to release. Craft-first storytelling where the visual world is the product demo.

## Why it worked

Craft as message: the extraordinary visual effects (practical sets extending CGI) demonstrated what music through HomePod feels like — not what it sounds like. The film was shared as entertainment, not advertising. Spike Jonze's authorship gave it cultural permission to exist at feature-film quality.

## Steal

When you cannot demo a sensory experience (sound, smell, touch) through traditional advertising, use visual metaphor at cinematic quality. The visual must match the ambition of the claim.

# iPod Silhouettes (Apple · 2004)

## Insight
The iPod changed the experience of music in public — for the first time, everyone on a city street could be living inside a completely private concert while physically present in a crowd. The silhouette is the perfect visual metaphor for that interior-exterior split: present but elsewhere, public but private.

## Mechanic
Solid-color backgrounds (hot pink, electric blue, lime green) with black silhouettes of people dancing, headphone cord visible, earbuds in. No faces. No demographic signals. Pure movement, pure energy, pure color. The design decision (P17) was the concept: silhouettes made the product universal (any person, any age, any culture) and made the music experience visible. The campaign extended across TV, outdoor, print, and became one of the most recognizable visual systems in advertising history.

## Why it worked
P17 (design as idea) at its highest level: the creative decision — black silhouette on saturated color — was the entire argument. No copy. No feature list. No demographic targeting. The silhouette communicated "this could be you" more effectively than any representation of an actual person could, because the audience was invited to project themselves onto it. The campaign was also functionally perpetual: any song, any silhouette, any color. The design system was the brief.

## Steal
When your product changes how people experience the world rather than what they own, find a visual metaphor for the experience rather than the product. A design solution that removes all demographic specificity can reach more people than any targeted representation.

# Misunderstood (Apple · 2013)

## Insight
Parents and grandparents watching teenagers stare at their phones during family gatherings feel that technology is pulling the family apart. The cultural accusation against smartphones — that they make people antisocial — was acute. Apple's truth-telling move: what if the teenager is creating something meaningful for the family, not retreating from it?

## Mechanic
A 90-second Christmas film: a teenage boy appears distracted by his iPhone throughout family holiday activities. At Christmas morning, he plays a video on the TV — a beautifully edited film he's been making the entire time, documenting the family's gathering. Everyone is moved. The iPhone's camera and editing capability are demonstrated without a single product feature mentioned. P16 (long form) allows the emotional reversal to land; P13 (truth telling) names the anti-smartphone cultural accusation and inverts it with specific, emotional evidence.

## Why it worked
The cultural tension Apple was addressing (technology isolates families) was real and widespread. Rather than denying it, the campaign acknowledged it and then demonstrated the opposite — through a genuine emotional story, not a product claim. The iPhone becomes the instrument of connection, not disconnection. "Shot on iPhone" was implicit before it became a campaign. The final card — "Misunderstood" — was the strategic move: reclaiming the word used against Apple users. Cannes Grand Prix. Genuinely earned.

## Steal
When your brand is accused of a cultural harm, don't defend — demonstrate the exact opposite through a specific, emotionally credible story. The accusation creates the tension; the demonstration creates the resolution. Never argue; show.

# Shot on iPhone (Apple · 2015)

## Insight

Apple needs to prove the iPhone camera is professional-quality. Every competitor shows their own polished product shots. What if the proof is the users' own photography, at global scale?

## Mechanic

Real user photos and videos selected from millions of submissions, placed on billboards, digital OOH, and media worldwide with the credit "Shot on iPhone by [name]." Users become the campaign.

## Why it worked

User as co-author meets design as idea: UGC elevated to premium media placement changed the status of ordinary photos. The OOH context made amateur photography feel like gallery work. The brand disappeared — the proof took over. Running for 10+ years as a platform, not a campaign.

## Steal

UGC placed on premium media changes the perceived status of that UGC. The medium legitimizes the content. If your product enables creation, make creators the campaign rather than demonstrating the product yourself.
