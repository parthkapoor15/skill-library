# Factory (Skittles · 2009)

## Insight
The origin of Skittles — where do they come from? The "Factory" spot presumably extends Skittles' absurdist logic to answer that question in characteristically impossible terms.

## Mechanic
A short absurdist film set in or involving a factory in Skittles' surrealist creative framework. Exact execution details require source verification.

## Why it worked
Factory as concept provides Skittles' absurdism with a new arena: origin mythology. The brand's internal logic can be applied to answer "where do they come from?" in a way that extends rather than explains.

## Steal
Brand origin stories don't need to be factual to be effective. An absurdist origin that follows brand logic can be more memorable and more brand-building than a true story told plainly.

# Hide & Seek (Skittles · 2008)

## Insight
TODO: Verify exact year. Part of Skittles' "Touch the Rainbow" campaign — a sustained absurdist platform that ran from the mid-2000s through the 2010s.

## Mechanic
A Skittles spot using hide-and-seek as the premise for an absurdist scenario — likely involving the transformation of objects into Skittles or an unexpected twist on the childhood game delivered with deadpan sincerity.

## Why it worked
The campaign's power came from consistency: every execution used a different absurd setup but the same straight-faced commitment. The "hide and seek" framing provides a familiar structure that the brand then fills with completely illogical content.

## Steal
Use familiar social rituals (games, conversations, transactions) as scaffolding for absurd scenarios. The audience's familiarity with the ritual makes the departure from it funnier and sharper.

# Magic (Skittles · 2009)

## Insight
TODO: Verify exact year. Part of Skittles' sustained absurdist platform — one of a series of executions exploring transformation, surreal humor, and candy's illogical relationship with reality.

## Mechanic
A Skittles spot using magic or transformation as the absurdist premise — likely featuring an impossible transformation triggered by Skittles, played with complete deadpan sincerity and no explanation offered.

## Why it worked
Skittles was one of the few candy brands to build a consistent creative language out of pure absurdism across multiple years. The "magic" theme connects to the brand's candy-as-transformative-force territory that made spots like "Touch the Rainbow" culturally memorable.

## Steal
Transformation is a universally legible story structure. Absurdist brands can use transformation mechanics (A becomes B because of product) to create memorable visual moments that work without any rational product claim.

A short film in Skittles' established absurdist register, featuring a pinata scenario following the brand's surrealist internal logic. Exact execution details require source verification.

# Pineapple (Skittles · 2010)

## Insight
TODO: Verify exact year. Part of Skittles' ongoing "Touch the Rainbow" absurdist campaign era — a series of non-sequitur spots where absurd premises are committed to with total sincerity.

## Mechanic
A Skittles spot involving a pineapple in an absurdist scenario — likely following the brand's established pattern of taking a single surreal premise to its illogical extreme without explanation or irony.

## Why it worked
Skittles' campaign era created a consistent creative language: complete commitment to a single absurd premise, no winking at the camera, no explanation. The pineapple serves as the irrational object around which the brand's candy-colored surrealism operates.

## Steal
Absurdism works when the internal logic is consistent. Pick one absurd element and follow it faithfully — the comedy comes from the gap between ridiculous premise and straight-faced execution.
