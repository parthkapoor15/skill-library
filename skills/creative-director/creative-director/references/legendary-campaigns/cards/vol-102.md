# Stuck (Thorntons · 2009)

## Insight
Chocolate as an indulgence is typically marketed through sensory pleasure — slow melting, beautiful photography, gratification. Thorntons' positioning allowed for a different angle: the compulsive, uncontrollable pleasure of chocolate, taken to an absurd extreme.

## Mechanic
A Harmony Korine-directed spot (the director of "Kids," "Spring Breakers") — bringing arthouse sensibility to a confectionery brand. "Stuck" likely refers to a physical or emotional state of being unable to stop consuming, literalized through absurdist or surreal imagery. The auteur director choice signals that Thorntons was willing to make something genuinely unusual for the category.

## Why it worked
Hiring Harmony Korine to direct a chocolate ad is itself a P11 absurdist move — the mismatch between director and product creates intrigue before a single frame is seen. The long-form format (P16 element) allowed for the kind of unsettling, non-commercial visual grammar that Korine specializes in, which would be impossible in a 30-second format.

## Steal
Director-as-message: the choice of creative collaborator is sometimes the most significant creative decision. Hiring a director whose visual language is incompatible with the category creates friction that generates attention and distinctiveness.

# Dance Pony (Three UK · 2013)

## Insight
The "stuff that the internet was made for" platform: the internet exists for the joyful, pointless, and wonderful — not for productivity. A moonwalking Shetland pony in a field is a perfect embodiment of internet joy.

## Mechanic
A 60-second film of a Shetland pony moonwalking in a field to Fleetwood Mac's "Everywhere." No copy. No features. Just a pony dancing. Preceded by the campaign line: "This is what three uses our network for. Enjoy." Went viral immediately and generated millions of organic views.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) at its purest — a brand spending broadcast budget on a dancing pony is the most direct demonstration of "we're here for the good stuff on the internet." The absurdism is not separate from the message; it is the message. Zero features communicated; maximum brand character transferred.

## Steal
The purest version of an absurdist brand character is spending your media budget on the exact thing you're celebrating. The ad is the argument.

# Phones Are Good (Three UK · 2018)

## Insight
Every telecom brand communicates as if smartphones are tools for genuine human connection. The cultural discourse, meanwhile, treats smartphones as a source of addiction, distraction, and disconnection. Three UK took the side the industry never takes: phones are, actually, good.

## Mechanic
A series of absurdist vignettes celebrating the genuinely useful and pleasurable things people do with phones — delivered with the straight-faced absurdist tone that Wieden+Kennedy London had developed for the brand. Counter-intuitive because it says something the entire industry knows but everyone is too earnest to state.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) combined with a genuinely contrarian brand position: in a cultural moment of phone-shaming, Three UK said "no, phones are great" and meant it. The campaign's confidence is the differentiator — most telecom brands hedge their message with responsibility disclaimers. Three owned the pro-phone position completely.

## Steal
When your entire category is walking away from its core product benefit under cultural pressure, own the benefit more aggressively than ever. The brand that doesn't apologise for what it sells stands out in a category of apologetic competitors.

# It's a Tide Ad (Tide · 2018)

## Insight

Every Super Bowl ad featuring clean clothes, people looking good, or domestic happiness is implicitly an ad for Tide — even if it's advertising a car, beer, or perfume. Tide owns the visual of clean.

## Mechanic

Series of Super Bowl spots that begin looking like ads for other categories (beer, luxury cars, cologne) before David Harbour from Stranger Things appears and announces "This is a Tide Ad." Every Super Bowl ad with clean clothes becomes suspect. Cultural hijack of the entire advertising break.

## Why it worked

Format as idea meets cultural hijack meets absurdism: the campaign used the meta-awareness of the Super Bowl advertising environment as the creative territory. By claiming all clean-clothes imagery as Tide's domain, they occupied the entire break — not just their own spots. The format (appearing in other ads) was the idea.

## Steal

Find the category property your brand legitimately owns that nobody has explicitly claimed. Build a campaign that claims it loudly. The claim itself is the disruption.

# Talking Stain (Tide · 2008)

## Insight
A stain at the wrong moment doesn't just embarrass you — it actively sabotages you. It talks over you, drowns you out, undermines everything you say. The stain is not just a problem; it's an adversary with an agenda.

## Mechanic
Super Bowl spot: a man in a stain-covered shirt gives a job interview. Every time he speaks, the stain itself speaks louder — gargling, babbling, completely overwhelming his words. The interviewer only hears the stain. Tide presented as the solution to this betrayal.

## Why it worked
Giving the stain a voice turned an abstract anxiety (looking unprofessional) into a vivid enemy with agency. The premise was absurd but emotionally true — a stain really does speak for you. The Super Bowl context amplified the stakes and the comedy simultaneously.

## Steal
Abstract anxieties become unforgettable when personified as antagonists with specific agendas. The stain didn't just embarrass; it actively sabotaged. Find the enemy your product eliminates and give it a personality.
