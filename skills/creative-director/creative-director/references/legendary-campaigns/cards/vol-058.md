# FCK (KFC · 2018)

## Insight
When KFC ran out of chicken in the UK — a chicken restaurant with no chicken — no PR playbook existed for the category of failure. The only credible response was the one that acknowledged the absurdity of the situation at the same volume as the outrage.

## Mechanic
A full-page newspaper ad: the KFC bucket with the letters rearranged to "FCK." One line of body copy acknowledging the situation with genuine humility and characteristic brand wit. No hedging, no corporate-speak, no blame. Just the bucket, the rearranged letters, and an honest apology that made the audience laugh and forgive simultaneously.

## Why it worked
P05 (cultural hijack) of their own crisis — KFC turned the worst news day in their brand history into their most celebrated creative work. P02 (enemy): the enemy here is corporate crisis communication convention, which KFC obliterated by being the opposite. The bucket visual — the most recognisable element of the brand — used against itself. One design decision (FCK) did everything the copy could not. Black Pencil and Cannes Grand Prix confirmed this as one of the most efficient single acts of brand communication ever made.

## Steal
The crisis response that wins is not the managed apology but the one that meets the audience's emotional register exactly. Anger and absurdity require absurdity and honesty in return — not careful corporate language. The brand that can laugh at itself in a genuine emergency earns permanent goodwill.

# The Return of the Colonel (KFC · 2015)

## Insight
KFC had become a brand living off a dead founder's legacy without acknowledging it. The Colonel was everywhere — on every bucket, every sign — but nowhere in the brand's actual communication. The gap between heritage and behaviour was the comedy.

## Mechanic
KFC reintroduced Colonel Sanders as an active character played by rotating celebrities (Darrell Hammond, Norm Macdonald, Jim Gaffigan, Rob Lowe, Reba McEntire, and others), each delivering a distinct comedic persona. The absurdist campaign had no continuity between performers — the same character played by wildly different people was the joke.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) built around the structural irony of a brand icon played by rotating strangers. P05 (cultural hijack) borrowed the fame of unexpected celebrity choices — each casting was itself a PR moment. The campaign self-consciously made the Colonel's unreality the content, which paradoxically made him more present in culture than the previous decade of passive logo usage.

## Steal
If your brand already has an icon it doesn't use, reactivating it ironically — acknowledging the absurdity of the icon itself — is more honest and more entertaining than treating legacy seriously. The meta-awareness is the differentiator.

# Portraits Completed (Kiwi · 2018)

## Insight
Iconic portraits throughout history show subjects from head to toe — but the shoes are often cut off by the frame. The one element that Kiwi polishes is systematically excluded from the most famous images in art history.

## Mechanic
Kiwi extended famous historical portraits — Mona Lisa, American Gothic, Girl with a Pearl Earring — below the original frame to reveal the subjects' shoes, polished to perfection. The extensions were executed in the style of the originals by skilled illustrators.

## Why it worked
The design insight is the entire idea: the product exists in the missing part of the frame. No copy needed — the implication (that great people wore great shoes, now revealed) communicates the brand's role without stating it. The executions generate curiosity about what is below any frame.

## Steal
Find the famous images in your world that systematically exclude your product's territory. Extend them into that territory. Let the implication do the work.

# Concert Milk (Konzerthaus Dortmund · 2015)

## Insight
Classical music is perceived as élite and intimidating to those who haven't grown up with it. The Konzerthaus wanted to reach people in their daily routines — before they'd made the decision to not go.

## Mechanic
Milk cartons distributed through supermarkets functioned as miniature concert programs and recruitment tools — turning the everyday breakfast ritual into a point of contact with classical music. The carton design brought concert scheduling and artist information to a non-concert context.

## Why it worked
The design object traveled into a domestic, low-barrier space where classical music would never normally appear. The carton became a cultural ambassador, reaching people who would never pick up a brochure. The design did the work the advertising couldn't.

## Steal
When the audience won't come to you, design something that travels to them through an existing daily ritual. Find the mundane object that shares a context with the behavior you're trying to change.

# Evert_45 (KPN Telecom · 2016)

## Insight

Technology connects people across distance and time — but for elderly people who lived through WWII, this means something specific: they can connect with the next generation who has never experienced war, and those young people can learn from someone who has.

## Mechanic

KPN created a Twitter account for 91-year-old WWII veteran Evert Willemsz — staffed by KPN employees who helped him tweet daily in the 70 days leading up to Liberation Day. Young Dutch people discovered, followed, and replied. A bridge across generations built through a telecom platform.

## Why it worked

Tech as canvas meets user as co-author: the technology wasn't the hero — the human story was. But the technology (Twitter's real-time, public, participatory format) made the intergenerational conversation possible at scale. KPN demonstrated its platform's meaning rather than its features.

## Steal

Find someone whose story needs to be told, who cannot tell it through modern channels without help. Build the infrastructure for that story. Your brand is the bridge, not the storyteller.
