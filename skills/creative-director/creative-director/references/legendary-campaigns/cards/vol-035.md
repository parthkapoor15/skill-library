# Find Each Other (Facebook · 2021)

## Insight
Facebook launched amid a reputational crisis (post-Cambridge Analytica, mid-congressional hearings). A campaign about the platform's features would fail; a campaign about why human connection matters could reframe the conversation from "what Facebook does" to "why people need each other."

## Mechanic
Long-form films showing the moments when people who had lost touch with each other — by distance, by time, by circumstance — found each other again through the platform. The stories are human; the technology is invisible.

## Why it worked
Long-form drama (P16): the emotional credibility of connection stories requires time. The campaign positions the platform as infrastructure for human need rather than as a product. Invisible technology is the correct communication strategy when the technology itself is under attack — make the benefit visible, not the mechanism.

## Steal
When your brand is under reputational attack, the most defensible position is the one that argues from human need rather than brand feature. Show what people lose when the product disappears, not what the product does when present.

# Didn't Ship With FedEx (FedEx · 2002)

## Insight

The anxiety of a package not arriving is universal. FedEx's entire business model rests on making that anxiety go away. The most honest version of that value proposition: show what happens when you choose someone else.

## Mechanic

A TV comedy showing a series of escalating disasters stemming from not using FedEx for a critical delivery. The absurdist chain of consequences gives the brand a permission to be funny about what is genuinely a serious category concern. Part of FedEx's tradition of using humor to communicate reliability.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): absurdist consequence chains make stakes feel real without melodrama. The humor disarms the viewer while landing a genuine product argument. FedEx consistently proved that a reliable, serious category could be led by comedy — a differentiation choice that competitors found hard to follow because it required genuine craft.

## Steal

If your category is defined by anxiety (delivery, insurance, finance), comedy is the most efficient way to acknowledge the anxiety without amplifying it. The joke about failure reassures the audience that the brand understands the stakes.

# Fast Talker (FedEx · 1981)

## Insight

FedEx's "absolutely, positively overnight" promise was so compressed and specific that it needed to be communicated at the speed it described. The insight: make the ad itself a demonstration of urgency — a man who talks as fast as FedEx delivers.

## Mechanic

A TV commercial featuring John Moschitta Jr. — then the world's fastest talker — delivering a corporate dialogue scene at extraordinary speed, listing FedEx's services and benefits in under 60 seconds. The commercial ends before the viewer fully processes what happened. Moschitta's speed was itself the proof of concept: if the ad can move this fast, the delivery can too. The campaign won multiple awards and is widely credited with establishing FedEx's brand voice in its category.

## Why it worked

P14 (product as proof): the execution structure demonstrated the benefit rather than claiming it. P11 (absurd as carrier): a man talking at inhuman speed is inherently comic, and the comedy disarmed resistance while the information landed. The casting of a genuine speed-talking record holder gave the demonstration authenticity — it couldn't be dismissed as a trick. The commercial itself became a cultural reference point.

## Steal

If your product's core benefit is speed, efficiency, or precision — make the communication itself demonstrate that quality structurally. Don't claim speed; perform it.

# Most Dangerous Band in the World (FedEx · 2016)

## Insight
FedEx delivers dangerous goods — hazardous materials, explosives, chemicals — under strict regulations. The absurdist question: what would the most dangerous musical instrument delivery look like?

## Mechanic
A comedic long-form film exploring what happens when a band composed of the world's most dangerous musical instruments — presumably explosive, radioactive, or otherwise hazardous band equipment — needs to be shipped. FedEx is the only company capable of handling the shipment.

## Why it worked
Absurdism (P11) used to dramatise a genuine product attribute (FedEx's capacity to handle hazardous and complex shipments) through an escalating ridiculous scenario. The long-form structure (P16) allows the absurd premise to compound through multiple surreal complications before resolving on the FedEx solution.

## Steal
Dangerous or complex product attributes can be dramatised through the most extreme possible absurdist scenario requiring that capability. The scenario doesn't need to be realistic — it needs to be funny enough that the underlying capability registers.

# Stolen Idea (FedEx · 2006)

## Insight
Business culture runs on ideas — but ideas without execution are worthless. FedEx's comedy spots operated in the territory of business absurdism: what happens when things go wrong because someone didn't use FedEx.

## Mechanic
An absurdist comedy spot (P11) showing the consequences of an idea being "stolen" — likely within a corporate context — as a comedic demonstration of why reliable delivery matters. The enemy mechanic (P02) operates implicitly: unreliable logistics is the villain.

## Why it worked
FedEx's long-running campaign of office comedy spots (including the famous "caveman" and "Monday" ads) established a register of knowing, wry business humor. "Stolen Idea" extends this territory. The absurdism is grounded in recognizable business anxiety, making it specific enough to resonate with the B2B audience.

## Steal
B2B humor works when it targets the specific anxieties of the business audience — not generic workplace comedy but the precise fears (credit-stealing, unreliable systems, missed deadlines) that business people experience daily.
