# Last Orders (Stella Artois · 2000)

## Insight

The moment before "last orders" in a British pub concentrates everything worth loving about pub culture into a single beat of time. Stella Artois understood that what they were really selling was not beer but that irreplaceable social ritual.

## Mechanic

A cinematic TV film depicting the drama, comedy, and bittersweet emotion of pub closing time in the UK — the negotiations, the final gestures, the last-moment reconciliations. Part of the long-running "Reassuringly Expensive" creative territory.

## Why it worked

P16 (long-form drama) used the British pub as a stage for miniature human theater. P11 (absurd as carrier) introduced comic exaggeration within fundamentally recognizable social rituals. The humor made the nostalgia palatable; the nostalgia made the humor meaningful. Stella's positioning as a premium lager was reinforced by the quality of the storytelling itself.

## Steal

Find the ritual moment around your product where human nature is most compressed and legible. That moment — not the product — is the story. The product earns its place by being present at the ritual.

# Hardchorus (StubHub · 2014)

## Insight
Sports fans are performers themselves — the chant, the war paint, the stadium voice. Giving that performative energy a platform beyond the stadium validates the fan's identity as essential to the sport.

## Mechanic
A choir of die-hard sports fans singing operatic arrangements of stadium chants with full orchestral backing — absurdly grandiose production applied to raw fan energy. Users could submit their own chants to be included.

## Why it worked
The absurd contrast between opera-quality production and the raw, unpolished world of sports fandom created shareability while making fans feel genuinely celebrated. The UGC mechanic gave existing superfans a reason to engage and recruit.

## Steal
When your audience already performs (sings, chants, cheers), give that performance an absurdly elevated stage. The elevation itself is the compliment.

# Icons (The Sunday Times · 2014)

## Insight
The Sunday Times is a newspaper with a century of history covering the people who shaped Britain and the world — but readers don't feel that legacy as a lived dimension of the paper.

## Mechanic
A film showing iconic figures from history in vivid colour, presented as if photographed today — connecting the contemporary reader to the paper's historical witness. Visual style makes the past feel present.

## Why it worked
The craft of the film elevated the brand's historical role. By presenting history with cinematic presence, The Sunday Times asserted that journalism shapes how posterity sees icons — and that reading the paper places the reader in that tradition.

## Steal
Use the visual language of contemporary prestige photography to present archival or historical material. Removing temporal distance through craft makes institutional heritage feel alive rather than dusty.

# The Swedish Number (Swedish Tourist Association · 2016)

## Insight
Tourism advertising sells destinations through imagery and aspirational copy. Sweden's real competitive advantage was something money can't buy: the candor of ordinary Swedes. Instead of describing Sweden, you could talk to it.

## Mechanic
Sweden published a single phone number — +46 771 793 336 — that connected any caller in the world to a random Swedish volunteer. No script, no filter. You could ask anything. Over 197 countries called; 32,000+ Swedes signed up as ambassadors. Each conversation was its own PR story.

## Why it worked
User-as-co-author (P09): every volunteer became a live ambassador, every call a piece of earned media. Tech-as-canvas (P18): the telephone infrastructure became the campaign. The mechanic was intrinsically newsworthy — "country publishes national phone number" is a headline that writes itself. Authenticity was guaranteed because it was literally unscripted.

## Steal
When your product is an experience, give the audience access to the experience itself rather than a representation of it. A hotline to a real person beats a glossy film every time. The constraint (volunteers, unknown callers) becomes the proof of brand confidence.

# Play It Safe (Sydney Opera House · 2023)

## Insight
The Opera House is perceived as a venue for safe, established art — a place people feel they should go but don't because it feels intimidating or irrelevant. But the institution's actual programming is anything but safe.

## Mechanic
A full musical film written and performed by Tim Minchin: a sardonic song celebrating the choice to "play it safe" — get a safe job, date safe people, never take artistic risks — with the implicit argument that the Opera House is for people who refuse. Cinematic quality, no product exposition, irony as the primary register.

## Why it worked
An institution using self-satire to challenge its own perceived stuffiness is structurally unusual and almost impossible to ignore. Long-form drama (P16) gave the musical enough runtime to build genuine comedic momentum. Truth-telling (P13): naming the audience's actual anxiety about the venue directly rather than papering over it with aspirational imagery.

## Steal
Heritage institutions can use self-deprecating irony to dismantle the intimidation barrier — but only if the irony is genuine and the content is actually as good as the institution claims. The film must be better than what people expected.
