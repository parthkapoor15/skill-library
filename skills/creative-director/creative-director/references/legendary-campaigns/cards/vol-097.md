# Justino (Spanish Christmas Lottery · 2015)

## Insight
The Spanish Christmas Lottery is a national ritual — families, coworkers, entire villages buy tickets together. The emotional truth is not the prize; it's the shared anticipation and what it means to win alongside the people you love.

## Mechanic
Justino is a lonely night-shift worker at a mannequin factory who secretly creates playful scenes with the mannequins to delight his day-shift colleagues. When he wins the lottery, he shares his ticket with them — strangers made family by proximity and a gesture of generosity.

## Why it worked
The film works on the P16 long-form drama mechanic: a character with a clear desire (connection), obstacles (the night shift, invisibility), and a resolution that reframes the lottery from individual luck to collective joy. The mannequin world is visually distinctive. Justino is genuinely moving without sentiment.

## Steal
When you have a product associated with luck or chance, reframe it around generosity and community. The prize is not the point; what you do with it is.

# You May Not Get It (Sports Illustrated · 2005)

## Insight
Sports Illustrated is a magazine that understands sports culture on a level that casual observers cannot access — the inside knowledge, the history, the statistical literacy. Non-sports-readers couldn't understand the allure; devoted readers needed no explanation.

## Mechanic
Print and OOH campaign deploying absurdist, highly sports-specific humor designed to be completely opaque to non-fans and immediately hilarious to insiders. The tagline "You may not get it" was directed at non-fans — an inversion of the typical advertising impulse to broaden appeal. P17 (design as idea): the visual execution was spare, relying on typography and specific references rather than illustration or photography.

## Why it worked
By explicitly excluding non-fans, SI strengthened its position with the audience it actually served. "You may not get it" validated the insider identity of sports fans — their understanding of the references was a badge of belonging. The absurdism worked because it was specifically legible to sports fans who had the context; opaque absurdism would have failed.

## Steal
Exclusion can be a stronger loyalty signal than inclusion. When your audience has genuine insider knowledge, create work that rewards that knowledge and explicitly acknowledges that outsiders won't understand. The exclusion confirms that you are in the in-group.

# Spreadbeats (Spotify · 2025)

## Insight

Spotify's advertising pitch deck is a boring spreadsheet — the most uncreative format possible for the most creative medium. What if the pitch deck itself became the product demo?

## Mechanic

Spotify presented its advertising capabilities to media buyers as a fully interactive spreadsheet (Google Sheets) where every cell was a clickable music experience — the spreadsheet became a musical instrument demonstrating Spotify's audio targeting, playlist context, and emotional range.

## Why it worked

Tech as canvas meets design as idea: the format inversion (spreadsheet as music player) was the demo. B2B advertising typically ignores the format of its communication. By making the format the message, Spotify turned a sales document into a talked-about creative piece.

## Steal

In B2B contexts, the format of your pitch is almost always ignored. Find a format that is the opposite of what's expected, then make that format demonstrate the product. The surprise is disproportionate because expectations are so low.

# Calling JohnMalkovich.com (Squarespace · 2015)

## Insight

Anyone can build a website — including John Malkovich, who wants to design a fashion line. Squarespace's product democratizes presence that previously required infrastructure.

## Mechanic

Super Bowl spot: John Malkovich, furious that someone else owns JohnMalkovich.com, calls the squatter and pleads for his domain. The absurdity of a famous person not owning their own name online dramatized exactly what Squarespace solves.

## Why it worked

Absurd as carrier meets tech as canvas: the comic scenario had an exact logical connection to the product's real function (own your online presence). The celebrity's predicament was the product demo — without a demo.

## Steal

Cast someone famous in the exact situation your product solves. The irony of their status (famous person lacks digital presence) does the brief's work without stating the benefit.

# Little Lad (Starburst · 2007)

## Insight
Starburst's berry flavors needed a campaign moment that translated the intensity of fruit flavor into visual absurdism. The "berries and cream" spot found an unlikely ambassador: a Victorian-era little lad who goes into raptures over cream-flavored candy.

## Mechanic
A bizarre, committed performance by a little person dressed as a 19th century schoolboy, dancing and singing "berries and cream" with escalating enthusiasm. The spot committed fully to its absurd premise without winking. No explanation. No product shot held beyond what's necessary.

## Why it worked
The commitment was total — the actor was genuinely in the bit, not commenting on it. Skittles-adjacent absurdism applied to a non-Skittles brand: the specific historical/class incongruity of "berries and cream" as an aristocratic enthusiasm made it culturally quotable. The spot became a meme reference years after airing, resurfacing on TikTok.

## Steal
Commit completely to the absurd character, setting, or premise. Half-committed absurdism reads as awkward; fully committed absurdism reads as confident. The audience laughs with the ad, not at it.
