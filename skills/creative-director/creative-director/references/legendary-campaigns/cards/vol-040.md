# Kash (GEICO · 2012)

## Insight
GEICO's promise — "15 minutes could save you 15% or more" — is about money you keep rather than money you spend. Making that saved money a physical, present character turns an abstract benefit into something the audience can see and interact with.

## Mechanic
A stack of cash with googly eyes (literally "the money you could be saving") appears in domestic situations — watching TV with its owner, sitting at the breakfast table, resentfully ignored while the owner overpays elsewhere. Pure visual gag; pure product benefit made character.

## Why it worked
The googly-eye cash stack externalised the product's core promise as a character you could feel guilty ignoring. It was legally simple, visually distinctive, cheap to produce, and impossible to forget. The insight (you're ignoring money you could have) became a judgmental presence.

## Steal
Product benefits can be turned into characters who express emotions about your audience's behaviour. The "money you could be saving" as a neglected character looking reproachfully at the camera is a benefit articulation, not just a joke.

# Unskippable (GEICO · 2015)

## Insight
Viewers skip pre-roll ads at the five-second mark — so GEICO delivered the entire message in the first five seconds, then let the frozen, absurd aftermath play on.

## Mechanic
A series of YouTube pre-rolls where the brand message lands in the first five seconds; the actors then "freeze" while the world around them continues absurdly — a dog eating the table, a baby idol eating oatmeal. Title card: "You can't skip this GEICO ad because it's already over."

## Why it worked
The format constraint (skip button) became the concept itself. Viewers who skipped got the message; viewers who stayed were rewarded with surreal comedy. The limitation was reframed as structural confidence.

## Steal
When a format has a technical hard limit, make that limit load-bearing. Turn the constraint into the punchline rather than fighting it.

# Endless Possibilities (Getty Images · 2018)

## Insight
Stock photography has historically presented a narrow, homogeneous vision of what success, family, love, and business look like. Getty's library is vast enough to represent almost any version of human experience — but most buyers default to the clichéd images.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign using Getty's own image library as the creative material — showing the same narrative moments (a first day of work, a wedding, a child's birthday) in wildly diverse configurations drawn from real library assets. The campaign argued that representation in visual media is a choice, and Getty enables better choices.

## Why it worked
User as co-author (P09): the audience's use of Getty images downstream was the implicit extension of the campaign — every image buyer who chose a more representative image was participating in the campaign's purpose. Design as idea (P17): the visual language was the argument, demonstrated through real images rather than described in copy.

## Steal
When your product is visual content, use the product itself as the creative material. Don't describe what your library can do — show the range of stories it contains by telling the same story multiple ways through images that already exist in the catalogue.

# Endless Stories (Getty Images · 2019)

## Insight
Every image in Getty's library is a story that hasn't yet been told by the person who will eventually use it — the same photograph carries entirely different meanings depending on the context, the editorial decision, and the viewer.

## Mechanic
A film demonstrating how a single Getty image can be assigned radically different meanings, contexts, and stories depending on how it's used. A continuity of image through multiple narrative recontextualisations — the same face, the same scene, telling completely different stories.

## Why it worked
User as co-author (P09): the film made visible the creative power that every Getty customer actually holds — the ability to assign meaning to an image. The insight is about authorship: Getty doesn't sell pictures, it enables stories.

## Steal
Product brands that enable creative work can demonstrate the range of that enablement by showing the same asset transformed by different choices. The product's power is measured by the range of stories it can carry.

# Stick It (GoDaddy · 2013)

## Insight
GoDaddy built its brand in the early 2000s on Super Bowl controversy — provocative, body-centric spots that had nothing to do with domain registration. The absurdity was the point: make a boring tech utility famous by being the most talked-about ad in the room.

## Mechanic
A Super Bowl spot using absurdist physical comedy and non-sequitur humor to generate conversation around the GoDaddy brand. Shock and irreverence substituted for product explanation.

## Why it worked
In a category with near-zero emotional resonance (domain hosting), GoDaddy chose to not compete on product features but on pure cultural noise. The ads were designed to be criticized and debated, which kept the brand name in circulation. Controversy as earned media.

## Steal
When the category is boring and competitors all look the same, absurdist disruption can function as brand differentiation — but only if volume of conversation is the actual metric. This strategy trades credibility for fame.
