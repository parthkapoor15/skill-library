# The Talk (P&G · 2017)

## Insight
In America, Black mothers have a specific, decades-long tradition of preparing their children for encounters with the police — a conversation about how to behave so as not to be killed. Most white Americans don't know this conversation exists.

## Mechanic
A film showing Black mothers across multiple generations — 1960s, 1980s, 2000s, present — each having "The Talk" with their children: specific instructions about how to interact with police and authority in a way that keeps them safe. The conversations change in their wording but not in their necessity.

## Why it worked
P&G's brand platform is about "the best a person can be" across the family arc — a company with sufficient scale and cultural standing to address this without it being perceived as purely opportunistic. Social experiment (P12): the film doesn't editorialize or advocate; it shows a ritual so that viewers who have never heard of it can witness its reality. Long-form drama (P16): the generational structure required length to land the cumulative weight of the perpetuation across time.

## Steal
Universal themes like racism become specific and credible when reduced to a single, named, repeating ritual. "The Talk" is not about racism in general — it is about one specific conversation that proves the existence of a systemic problem without argument.

# Carousel (Philips · 2009)

## Insight
Philips Cinema 21:9 TV offered an unprecedented ultrawide format that no film or TV content had been produced to fill. The feature was technically impressive but visually abstract — without a demonstration, audiences couldn't understand what 21:9 meant experientially.

## Mechanic
A 4-minute cinematic short film created specifically for the 21:9 ratio, directed by Adam Berg. A frozen crime scene at a wedding — a single continuous camera move through a moment of chaos, suspended in time, circling around the frozen figures. The camera's movement revealed angles impossible in standard 16:9. The film was designed to exist only in the 21:9 format — viewing it in any other format destroys the composition.

## Why it worked
P08 (craft as message): the extraordinary technical craft of the frozen-moment execution was immediately legible as proof of cinematic capability — the TV that could display this was evidently different. P17 (design as idea): the 21:9 aspect ratio was the concept; the film existed to demonstrate that the ratio created something impossible in standard dimensions. The Cannes Grand Prix recognized what made this genuinely new: a product demonstration that was simultaneously a work of art that the product had made possible.

## Steal
When a new format or technology creates a capability that is invisible in standard demonstration, make content that can only exist because of that capability. The content is proof of concept and advertisement simultaneously.

# The Gallery of Emerging Species (Play-Doh · 2018)

## Insight
Children's imagination produces genuinely alien, absurdist creatures — neither fish nor fowl, belonging to no known taxonomy. Play-Doh's product is not a toy; it is a world-building material.

## Mechanic
Play-Doh collected children's creations and exhibited them in a real gallery framed as an exhibition of "emerging species" — complete with pseudo-scientific naming and taxonomy plaques. The absurd seriousness of the gallery format amplifies the joy of the creatures.

## Why it worked
The gallery context elevates children's work to art without condescension. The absurdist taxonomy makes adults laugh while validating children. The format — a real gallery, not a digital showcase — creates earned media and a genuine artifact worth visiting.

## Steal
Take your audience's output seriously enough to give it an institutional frame. The gap between the grandeur of the frame and the playfulness of the content IS the idea.

# Double Life (PlayStation · 1999)

## Insight

In 1999, gaming was still widely seen as juvenile — a hobby for children, not a serious cultural pursuit. But the people who played PlayStation were adults living a complete double life: accountants who slaughtered armies, nurses who commanded fleets, office workers who were assassins. This hidden identity was the most honest thing about the gaming experience — and the most interesting thing PlayStation could say about itself.

## Mechanic

A TV film directed by Frank Budgen: a montage of ordinary-looking people — an elderly woman, a policeman, a young mother — speaking directly to camera about their extraordinary gaming lives. "I have organized mutinies. I have led revolutions." "I have burned cities and saved lives." Spoken with complete sincerity and no irony. The contrast between speaker and statement builds to a final reveal of the PlayStation logo. No gameplay footage. No product demonstration.

## Why it worked

P13 (truth telling): the film stated a real cultural truth that the audience recognized instantly but had never heard spoken aloud by a brand. Gaming was a secret life for millions, and PlayStation was the first brand to say so with dignity rather than defensiveness. P16 (long-form drama) used the accumulation of testimony to build emotional weight — each speaker added to a collective portrait of a hidden world. Cannes Grand Prix 1999.

## Steal

When your product gives people a life they don't publicly acknowledge, name that life with full dignity. The audience that recognizes themselves in the description will feel understood in a way that standard product advertising never achieves.

# Gravity Cat (PlayStation · 2015)

## Insight

Gaming allows you to experience physics and realities that don't exist in the real world. PlayStation is the machine that makes the impossible feel real.

## Mechanic

Absurdist film and integrated campaign around a giant cat that defies gravity — the impossible physics of the game world made literal as a live-action spectacle. Cat scale and gravity inversion created a surreal visual world matching the game's reality-bending mechanics.

## Why it worked

Absurdism meets tech as canvas: the visual impossibility of the campaign content mirrored the visual impossibility of the game world it promoted. The absurd was not decoration — it was the brief being executed.

## Steal

When promoting a game or experience that defies real-world physics, make the campaign's visuals equally physics-defying. The medium should demonstrate what the product feels like.
