# Green Dawn (Ubisoft · 2020)

## Insight
When real-world events start to resemble video game plots, the line between fiction and reality blurs — and a gaming brand can exploit that blur for maximum attention.

## Mechanic
Ubisoft released a fake government PSA about an alien invasion — the type of absurdist premise central to their Far Cry: New Dawn game — as if it were a real public safety announcement, hijacking the format of official crisis communications.

## Why it worked
The stunt works on two levels: gamers in on the joke, non-gamers confused enough to share. The format inversion (official PSA for absurd content) is the mechanic. Speed of the reveal maintains audience trust.

## Steal
Borrow the most formal, trust-laden format in your target audience's world and fill it with something absurd. The gap between form and content is where attention lives.

# I Will What I Want (Under Armour · 2014)

## Insight
Female athletes are told — by experts, by culture, by their own families — what their bodies can and cannot do. The women who achieve despite this are not succeeding because of encouragement; they are succeeding in spite of rejection. That defiance is the product.

## Mechanic
Hero film featuring Misty Copeland, who was told at 13 she had the wrong body for ballet, performing the most physically demanding ballet she can — set against a rejection letter read aloud in voiceover. Extended across other athletes who rejected prescribed limits. Campaign drove a 28% increase in Under Armour women's line.

## Why it worked
Long-form drama (P16): the time needed to show the physical discipline and the emotional weight of the rejection letter simultaneously is earned by the film's structure. Truth telling (P13): Copeland's letter was real; her achievement was undeniable. The pairing of documented rejection with visible triumph is irrefutable. Vulnerable defiance is the tier-3 emotion — the strength comes from the admission of the obstacle, not its erasure.

## Steal
The most powerful female empowerment ad does not celebrate women as already powerful — it shows them becoming powerful despite specific named obstacles. The rejection letter is more effective than the medal. Specificity of the obstacle = universality of the aspiration.

# Rule Yourself (Under Armour · 2016)

## Insight
The public sees athletes only in moments of triumph. What they do not see is the thousands of hidden hours — the training at 4am, the repetition, the failure — that make the triumph possible. The athlete you see on the podium is the product of the athlete no one sees in the gym.

## Mechanic
Film featuring Michael Phelps training — in the pool, alone, before dawn — set to a haunting track. No competition footage. No gold medals. Only the invisible work. Premiered three days before the Rio Olympics. Became one of the most shared Olympic-adjacent campaigns ever.

## Why it worked
Long-form drama (P16): the slow burn — multiple minutes of underwater training footage — creates a meditative quality that implicates the viewer in the discipline. Product as proof (P14): Under Armour is the gear of the invisible hours; the product earns its place by being present in the authentic, unglamorous moment. The Olympics timing provided a context that made every frame more powerful.

## Steal
Show the version of success your audience never sees — the unglamorous, repetitive, private preparation. The gap between the hidden work and the public result is more emotionally powerful than either alone. Ugly training footage > victory footage.

# Tap Project (UNICEF · 2007)

## Insight
Access to clean tap water is so taken for granted in developed markets that people pay nothing for it at restaurants — while 4,000 children die daily for lack of it globally. The table you sit at is the simplest possible proximity to the problem.

## Mechanic
On World Water Day, restaurants across the US asked customers to donate $1 for the tap water they would normally receive free. Every dollar funded 40 days of clean water for one child. The menu insert was the entire campaign. Design as donation mechanism.

## Why it worked
The donation ask was proportional (a dollar), immediate (right now, at dinner), and experiential (you're holding the water as you read). No TV, no celebrity, no guilt trip — just a direct, elegant conversion of an everyday moment into a global act. The design of the program was the idea.

## Steal
When the gap between abundance (tap water at dinner) and scarcity (no water for children) is visible in the same moment, the donation is almost automatic. Find the physical moment where your audience's excess and someone else's absence coexist, and design the conversion in that room.
