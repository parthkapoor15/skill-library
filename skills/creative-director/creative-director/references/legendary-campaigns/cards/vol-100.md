# Meet Graham (Transport Accident Commission · 2016)

## Insight
Road safety campaigns rely on statistics and graphic footage — both of which audiences have learned to screen out. The reason people don't believe car crashes will kill them is that they have never seen what it would actually take for a human body to survive a crash.

## Mechanic
Working with trauma surgeon Christian Kenfield, road safety engineer David Logan, and artist Patricia Piccinini, TAC commissioned a life-size hyperrealistic sculpture of "Graham" — the only human form anatomically capable of surviving a car crash. Enlarged skull, ribcage that functions as a crumple zone, absent neck, reinforced knees. The sculpture toured Australian galleries and was accompanied by an interactive website.

## Why it worked
Design as idea (P17): the sculpture itself was the argument — more powerful than any statistic or piece of footage. Truth-telling (P13): rather than showing what a crash does to a person, it showed what a person would need to look like to be safe — an argument the audience had no defence against. Moving the campaign into gallery space gave it cultural legitimacy and audience self-selection.

## Steal
Convert a safety warning into an art object. When audiences have been numbed to data and shock footage, a physical artefact they choose to visit and engage with creates the engagement that interruption cannot.

# Nuns (Talens Rubber Cement · 1974)

## Insight

The adhesive category is functionally dull — everything sticks, claims are interchangeable. The only differentiator available is comedy. If rubber cement keeps things stuck together, the most absurd possible demonstration of that principle is a situation where things emphatically should not be stuck together.

## Mechanic

A TV commercial featuring nuns accidentally glued together or to objects by Talens rubber cement, in a comedic vignette. Part of the Dutch tradition of absurdist advertising that predated its widespread adoption in the UK and US. The religious setting heightens the incongruity and gives the comedy an irreverent edge.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): the combination of nuns, glue, and slapstick creates pure incongruity comedy. For a product with no meaningful point of differentiation, absurdism is the most defensible creative choice — it creates recall without requiring a credible claim. The irreverence was appropriate to a culture that had a tradition of comic advertising in otherwise dull categories.

## Steal

For commodity categories with no meaningful differentiation, absurdism is not a shortcut — it is the strategy. The joke is the memory. If nothing about your product is differentiated, make the advertising itself differentiated.

# Am I Typecast? (The Atlantic · 2016)

## Insight
The Atlantic is perceived as the magazine for elite coastal intellectuals — a perception that limits its audience and undermines its journalism's reach. Naming that stereotype with ironic self-awareness is more disarming than denying it.

## Mechanic
A comedic film in which The Atlantic actors/voices playfully interrogate whether the publication (and by extension its readers) is "typecast" — smart, ironic, and self-aware enough to mock the very label that might repel new readers while charming existing ones.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) through self-directed comedy; P13 (truth-telling) by naming the cultural positioning that the brand typically avoids addressing. Publications rarely acknowledge their own stereotypes — doing so signals a confidence that actually attracts the audience it risks repelling.

## Steal
If your brand carries a specific cultural stereotype — elitist, niche, too serious — address it before your audience does. Self-aware comedy is more persuasive than earnest repositioning because it proves you already understand the criticism.

# The Economist Print Ads (The Economist · 1988)

## Insight

The Economist's readers are defined by their willingness to hold an uncomfortable idea for long enough to understand it. The magazine's readers are not smarter than other people; they are more comfortable with complexity and more honest about their own knowledge gaps. The campaign's job was to make the intelligence aspiration feel attainable rather than intimidating, and to make the brand itself feel as witty and economical with language as the magazine's best writing.

## Mechanic

An ongoing OOH and print campaign using white Helvetica type on red backgrounds — no images, no photographs, no illustration. Just language, wit, and the Economist's distinctive visual language. Headlines include: "I never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42." "Would you like to sit next to you at dinner?" "It's lonely at the top, but at least there's something to read." The campaign ran for 30+ years with consistent visual identity and sharpening wit.

## Why it worked

P17 (design as idea): the template is the message — red, white, Helvetica, wit. The restraint communicates the brand's values (precision, intelligence, confidence) without a word of explanation. P11 (absurd as carrier): many executions use absurdist scenarios (the 42-year-old "management trainee") to deliver insights. P07 (invisible brand): no logo is needed — the visual language is so ownable that the brand is immediately recognizable from across the street.

## Steal

A design system that is completely owned by a single brand is worth more than any individual campaign. Build the template first; the executions are secondary. When the template is strong enough, any content placed within it reads as the brand.
