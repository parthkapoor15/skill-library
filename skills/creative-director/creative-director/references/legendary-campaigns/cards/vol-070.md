# Mayo (Miller Genuine Draft · 2000)

## Insight
American beer culture is built on occasion and ritual — not taste. The most interesting material for beer advertising is not the beer itself but the people who drink it and what they believe.

## Mechanic
A short Errol Morris character portrait featuring an individual with an unusual or strongly held opinion — possibly about mayonnaise or food — delivered with the quiet sincerity of a documentary interview. Part of the Miller Genuine Draft Errol Morris series.

## Why it worked
The specificity of opinion (even a banal one) creates a distinctly human portrait that is more memorable than a product demonstration. Morris's framing technique — interviewees speaking directly to camera — creates intimacy that advertising rarely achieves.

## Steal
Commission a documentarian, not an advertising director, for a character-led campaign. The grammar of documentary creates a different contract with the viewer: they believe what they see in ways they cannot believe a scripted commercial.

# Noah (Miller Genuine Draft · 2000)

## Insight
Beer advertising in the late 1990s was dominated by laddish humor and simplistic lifestyle imagery. Miller Genuine Draft commissioned director Errol Morris to apply his documentary portrait style to everyday American characters, creating something warmer and stranger than the category norm.

## Mechanic
A short character study — part of Errol Morris's Miller Genuine Draft campaign — featuring a real or real-feeling American persona in an intimate, slightly absurd conversation format. The beer functions as ambient context rather than hero product.

## Why it worked
Morris's documentary aesthetic imported the credibility of non-fiction filmmaking into beer advertising. The characters feel found rather than cast. The result is genuinely human and unexpected in a category built on archetypes.

## Steal
When the category uses fictional archetypes, recruit documentary aesthetics. Real people behave in ways actors can't predict — and audiences read the difference. The brand benefit is transferred through the authenticity of the format, not through a claim.

# Let's Motor (MINI Cooper · 2002)

## Insight
American cars in 2002 were getting bigger — SUV culture was at its peak. MINI was a small European car entering a market that celebrated excess. Most brands would apologize for smallness or try to compete on American terms. MINI could make smallness the joke, the stance, and the identity.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign built around the absurdity of a tiny car in an oversized country. A MINI was strapped to the roof of a Ford Excursion SUV and driven around America. OOH featured headlines like "The SUV backlash officially starts now." Print, outdoor, and events celebrated the car's smallness as a philosophical position rather than a limitation. Tagline: "Let's Motor."

## Why it worked
CP+B understood that MINI couldn't win on American car-buying criteria, so they changed the criteria. The campaign turned the size deficit into a cultural statement at the exact moment anti-SUV sentiment was building. The absurdism (MINI on an SUV roof) was a visual argument that needed no copy. Buyers who chose MINI felt they were choosing a position, not just a vehicle.

## Steal
When you enter a market where your product loses by established metrics, change the metrics. Find the cultural moment where the establishment's strength becomes its liability. Put your weakness on the roof of their strength — literally or metaphorically.

# The DNA Journey (Momondo · 2016)

## Insight

Nationalist pride and prejudice are built on the fiction of ethnic purity. DNA testing reveals that no one is only from where they think they are — we are all genetically mixed, and travel reveals the same truth.

## Mechanic

Social experiment: people with strong national or ethnic identities take DNA tests on camera. Their results — unexpected genetic heritage from countries they'd stereotyped or opposed — are revealed in real-time emotional reaction. Momondo positions itself as the brand of open exploration.

## Why it worked

Social experiment meets long-form drama: the DNA results functioned as undeniable evidence that the audience's prejudices couldn't argue with. The emotional reactions were genuine, unscripted, and structurally guaranteed — anyone's DNA disproves their nationalism. The brand as facilitator of discovery rather than claimant.

## Steal

Find a scientific truth that directly contradicts your audience's most confidently held belief. Design an experiment that reveals it experientially rather than arguing against it. Evidence beats argument every time.

# Moose (Monster.com · 1999)

## Insight
Adults rarely tell children the truth about work: that most people end up in jobs they tolerate rather than jobs they love. Hearing that truth from a child's innocent mouth is unbearable — and galvanising.

## Mechanic
Super Bowl spot featuring children earnestly describing their future careers: "I want to be a yes-man," "I want to be forced into early retirement." Delivered with complete sincerity by kids who have no idea what they're saying. Monster.com presented as the escape route.

## Why it worked
The absurdism came from tonal contrast — adult cynicism in children's voices — not from surreal content. Every viewer instantly recognised the adult they'd become or feared becoming. The humour was the wound and the warning simultaneously.

## Steal
The most effective use of children in advertising is not innocence — it's the contrast between their sincerity and the adult truth they accidentally reveal. What does your audience know about themselves but refuse to say out loud?
