# Time Upon a Once (GE · 2015)

## Mechanic
A fairy-tale film format applied to industrial innovation — narrated as a fable, scored as epic cinema, following the lifecycle of a GE technology from concept to world-changing application. The contrast between the humble "once upon a time" register and the genuine scale of industrial transformation produced unexpected emotional resonance.

## Insight
B2B brands rarely earn emotional attention because they speak in ROI language. GE's actual work — jet engines, wind turbines, medical imaging — is genuinely epic in human terms. The barrier is tone, not content.

## Why it worked
The fairy-tale register disarmed B2B skepticism. By treating engineers as heroes and turbines as dragons slain, GE translated industrial achievement into the oldest emotional grammar humans have: the story. Craft execution matched the ambition.

## Steal
For B2B brands with genuinely epic products, the problem is never content — it's register. Industrial achievements told in financial language are inert. The same achievements told in narrative grammar become cinema.

# Wall (GE · 2016)

## Insight
Talking to entities that cannot respond — machines, walls, institutions — is a universal frustration. GE's technology makes machines capable of responding, monitoring, and communicating. The joke about "talking to a wall" becomes an ironic point of departure.

## Mechanic
A film built around the absurdist premise of literally talking to a wall — extended to a point where the wall begins to respond, reflect, or reveal some unexpected capability. Part of GE's series with BBDO exploring industrial connectivity through absurdist scenarios.

## Why it worked
Absurdism (P11) derived from a cultural idiom ("like talking to a wall") taken literally and then inverted — the wall that talks back is GE's connectivity technology made concrete. Long-form format (P16) allows the absurd premise to breathe and accumulate surreal logic before arriving at the punchline.

## Steal
Advertising clichés and cultural idioms can be inverted literally to produce fresh absurdist premises. "Like talking to a wall" → make the wall talk. The audience's familiarity with the source creates immediate comprehension of the inversion.

# More GEICO Campaigns (GEICO · 2020)

## Insight
Insurance is the most avoided, least engaged-with category in advertising. GEICO's counter-thesis: if you make insurance advertising genuinely funny — not "insurance funny" but actually funny — people will watch voluntarily and share organically.

## Mechanic
A sustained multi-character, multi-format absurdist advertising platform across Gecko, Cavemen, Kash, Hump Day Camel, and numerous one-off executions. Each character owned distinct tonal territory while remaining under the GEICO brand umbrella. The platform's depth — 20+ years of irreverent execution — made any single GEICO ad a brand signal, not just an impression.

## Why it worked
No single GEICO character or campaign explains the brand's effectiveness — the accumulated signal of consistent, genuinely funny absurdism over decades does. The brand built tonal equity rather than claim equity. "GEICO" now means "unexpectedly funny insurance" regardless of which character appears.

## Steal
Tonal brand equity compounds. A single funny ad is an impression. Twenty years of consistent tonal commitment is a cultural position. Start early, commit fully, rotate characters and formats — but never rotate the tone.

# Cavemen (GEICO · 2004)

## Insight
The phrase "so easy a caveman could do it" — intended as praise for simplicity — is unconsciously offensive if cavemen actually exist and are perfectly sophisticated. Social slights delivered through careless idiom are invisible to the person using them and deeply felt by the person targeted.

## Mechanic
A long-running campaign featuring modern, fully civilised cavemen living contemporary professional lives who are repeatedly, casually offended by GEICO's "so easy a caveman could do it" slogan. Their reaction is not rage — it's the quiet, exhausted indignation of someone used to casual prejudice. The campaign spawned a network television show.

## Why it worked
The insight was a genuine inversion: the cavemen were the most socially aware characters in every scene. Their sense of injury was specific, recognisable, and genuinely funny because it captured the particular tone of casual prejudice — not dramatic but persistent. The campaign worked as comedy and as social observation simultaneously.

## Steal
The richest territory for brand absurdism is social commentary wearing comedy as a disguise. The cavemen weren't just funny — they were a specific observation about how progressive self-image and casual prejudice coexist. The brand claim became the punchline and the problem simultaneously.

# Hump Day (GEICO · 2013)

## Insight
Wednesday is "Hump Day" — the weekly nadir before the weekend relief. Insurance advertising is also a hump: a necessary, joyless purchase. GEICO's consistent strategy is to make the joyless joyful — and a camel loudly celebrating Wednesday is purely, inexplicably joyful.

## Mechanic
An office scene. A camel wanders through, announcing "HUMP DAYYY!" to indifferent employees. Pure non-sequitur. No direct product connection beyond the GEICO brand. The camel became a cultural meme, shared independently of any media buy.

## Why it worked
GEICO's brand permission — pure absurdism in service of making insurance less dreadful — was broad enough to make a wordplay-based camel film feel on-brand. The sharability came from the camel's pure, unexplained enthusiasm. People shared it because it made them happy, not because they engaged with GEICO.

## Steal
When a brand's territory is "relief from joylessness," pure joy with no product tether can work — but only if the brand already owns that tonal territory. GEICO had earned the right to make inexplicably funny ads. Without that equity, the camel is just a non-sequitur.
