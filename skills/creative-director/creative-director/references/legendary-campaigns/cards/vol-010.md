# Unhate (Benetton · 2011)

## Insight
Political enemies share more humanity than their public conflict suggests. The photographic image of enemy leaders kissing — an act of reconciliation — is impossible in reality but imaginable in design. If hatred is a learned behavior, "unhate" is the counter-program.

## Mechanic
A series of photomontage images showing world leaders who are publicly opposed kissing each other on the lips — Barack Obama and Hu Jintao, Nicolas Sarkozy and Angela Merkel, Benjamin Netanyahu and Mahmoud Abbas, Pope Benedict XVI and Ahmed Mohamed el-Tayeb. The images were released as global billboard campaign and online simultaneously. Pope image pulled after Vatican protest. The controversy generated tens of millions of media impressions.

## Why it worked
P17 (design as idea): the photomontage is the idea — no copy needed beyond the campaign word "Unhate." The design decision (compositing world leaders kissing) is itself the message. P13 (truth telling): the images say something true and uncomfortable about the performative nature of political enmity while presenting an impossible image. Benetton had the authentic brand standing to do this — decades of provocative social campaigns made this credible rather than opportunistic.

## Steal
Find the image that is impossible in reality but logically necessary as a statement. The world leaders kissing cannot happen in reality — which is precisely why it is effective as a designed image. The design gap between reality and the image is where the message lives.

# Dads in Briefs (BGH · 2022)

## Insight

Air conditioners are a rational purchase — nobody finds the category exciting. But sweaty summer heat is something everyone dreads, and the most relatable victims of heat are dads who suffer without air conditioning.

## Mechanic

OOH print campaign featuring sweaty middle-aged dads in briefs — the most unappealing, authentic image of what life without air conditioning looks like — as the hero image. No aspirational lifestyle. Just the honest, uncomfortable consequence of not buying the product.

## Why it worked

Absurdism meets design as idea: the anti-aspirational visual was the idea. In a category full of cool blue imagery and family comfort scenes, a sweating dad in underwear was impossible to ignore. The ugliness was the argument.

## Steal

In rational categories with interchangeable products, find the most honest negative consequence of NOT having your product. Make that consequence the hero image. Anti-aspiration is more memorable than aspiration.

# Decode Jay-Z (Bing · 2010)

## Insight
Search is only meaningful when you're looking for something that matters to you. Jay-Z's autobiography was a cultural event — hiding its pages across the world turned Bing's search capability into the tool you needed to find something you genuinely wanted.

## Mechanic
Pages of Jay-Z's memoir "Decoded" were hidden in real locations around the world — on pizza boxes, pool table felt, billboard-sized prints in Miami — each photographed and released as a Bing search challenge. Find the location using Bing, win a prize. The city clues matched content in the book. A scavenger hunt using real geography as media.

## Why it worked
Every element served dual duty: the book content provided the clues, the real-world locations created photography, Bing provided discovery, and users did the sharing. The campaign demonstrated Bing's search capability through desire, not instruction. You searched because you wanted the next page, not because Bing asked you to.

## Steal
The most powerful product demonstration is one where the user never notices they're being demonstrated to. Engineer a situation where someone pursues a goal they already have, and your product is the only route to it.

# Go Back to Africa (Black & Abroad · 2020)

## Insight

"Go back to Africa" is a racist slur used against Black people in the West — but Black & Abroad is a Black travel company specializing in African travel. The slur is actually an invitation.

## Mechanic

Black & Abroad used AI to monitor racist "Go back to Africa" tweets, then replied to each with stunning photography and video of the exact African destination that tweet inadvertently suggested. The racist slur became a travel recommendation engine.

## Why it worked

Behavior inversion meets user participation: the most hostile phrase directed at Black people was reclaimed as the brand's own tagline by reversing its intent. The AI-powered response system scaled the reclamation while the photography made Africa desirable. Racists became unwilling brand distributors.

## Steal

Find the most hostile thing said about your audience. Ask what it would mean if you took it literally and positively. Reclamation at scale — especially when automated — turns opponents into amplifiers.

# The Black Elevation Map (Black & Abroad · 2023)

## Insight

Black travelers face safety concerns and social hostility that other travelers don't. Standard travel tools (Google Maps, TripAdvisor) are built by and for the default traveler, with no information about where Black travelers are welcomed, celebrated, or at risk.

## Mechanic

Black & Abroad built a crowd-sourced interactive map where Black travelers rated destinations, businesses, and experiences by safety, welcome, and Black cultural richness — a parallel travel infrastructure built for and by Black travelers.

## Why it worked

Tech as canvas meets design as idea: the product was genuinely useful, not just symbolic. By building infrastructure that didn't exist, Black & Abroad created something that competitors (general travel brands) could not duplicate without building from scratch with the wrong audience.

## Steal

Find the data infrastructure your audience needs that no existing platform provides because they were not built with your audience in mind. Build it. Own the data. Become the authority for that audience's decisions.
