# Nature is Speaking (Conservation International · 2014)

## Insight

Humans talk about "saving the planet" — but the planet doesn't need saving. Humans do. Nature existed for billions of years before us and will continue without us. The conservation conversation has been framed from the wrong perspective.

## Mechanic

Series of films voiced by A-list celebrities (Julia Roberts as The Ocean, Harrison Ford as The Rainforest, Kevin Spacey as The Rainforest) speaking as nature itself — cold, ancient, indifferent to human survival. Nature isn't asking for help; it's issuing a warning.

## Why it worked

Radical truth-telling in a category built on guilt and hope: environmental messaging always frames nature as fragile and humans as responsible stewards. This inverted the power relationship entirely — nature as force that will outlast us, humans as the vulnerable party. The celebrity voice casting was paradoxically dehumanizing, making nature feel genuinely alien and powerful.

## Steal

Find the assumption that your entire category builds its messaging on. Ask what happens if you invert it completely — not for shock, but for truth. Reframe who needs whom.

# Baby Stroller Test-Ride (Contours · 2023)

## Insight

Parents buy strollers based on specs and reviews — but they never experience what it actually feels like to be the baby inside. The most important user's perspective is the one never consulted.

## Mechanic

Contours built full-size baby strollers that adults could ride in — and let expectant parents test-ride them to experience stroller quality from the baby's perspective. The role-reversal made product quality tangible in a completely novel way.

## Why it worked

Truth telling meets user as co-author: the insight (the baby's experience is the purchase decision's actual subject) was revealed through participation rather than stated. Adults in baby strollers was inherently shareable and press-worthy.

## Steal

Find the primary user of your product who is not the purchaser. Design an experience that makes the purchaser take that user's perspective. The perspective-shift becomes the product demo.

# Lights Out (Coors Light · 2024)

## Insight
The Coors Light "train mountains turn blue when cold" mechanic had become one of the most recognizable temperature indicators in the category — when a live power outage darkened an arena, the brand had a uniquely relevant cultural moment to hijack.

## Mechanic
When power went out at a major live sporting event, Coors Light activated immediately with creative referencing "Lights Out" — their own visual identity — turning an unplanned blackout into brand content with uncanny timing.

## Why it worked
The brand had built enough visual equity around the "lights/cold" mechanic that a literal lights-out moment felt like it belonged to them. The speed of response and the pre-existing brand language made it feel predestined rather than opportunistic.

## Steal
Build visual assets and pre-approved reactive concepts around predictable cultural failure modes (power outages, weather events, delays) before they occur. When they do, speed of activation plus brand fit creates the illusion of destiny.

# Beach (Corona · 2017)

## Insight
Beer brand communication lives in one of two registers — aspirational lifestyle fantasy or self-aware irony. Corona's beach mythology is so established it can be gently mocked from within, which is more charming than either earnest execution.

## Mechanic
A comedic film using Corona's own beach-and-sunshine iconography as the raw material for absurdist scenarios — taking the brand's established visual language to its logical extreme with a comedic twist that acknowledges the artifice.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) applied to a brand that already owns a strong visual territory — the absurdism works because the baseline is well-established. Self-referential humour from a brand with a clear visual identity is more credible than the same approach from a brand without one.

## Steal
When your brand has a visual territory so established it is almost a cliché, the next creative move is to inhabit that cliché with full awareness. The brand that knows it owns a trope can play with the trope — the brand that doesn't know it can't.

# Karate (Corona · 2017)

## Insight
Part of the same Corona absurdist series — the brand's relaxation mythology colliding with unexpected physical disciplines. Karate's formality and aggression are the perfect opposite of Corona's ease and passivity.

## Mechanic
Absurdist film placing Corona's sensory language alongside karate — the incongruity of beach relaxation and martial discipline played for comic effect within the brand's visual world.

## Why it worked
Execution within a series built on a single absurdist engine: Corona's established mythology + incongruous context = comedy. The further the displacement from beach relaxation, the greater the contrast and the sharper the brand reinforcement.

## Steal
Series comedy benefits from escalating incongruity — each entry should push the contrast further than the last. Map your brand's core sensory territory, then find the most opposite context and put them in the same frame.
