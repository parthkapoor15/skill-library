# Betty White (Snickers · 2010)

## Insight
"You're not you when you're hungry" works as an insight because hunger makes you something embarrassing — irritable, helpless, incapable. But the most powerful version of that is not "you become annoying" — it's "you become the wrong person entirely."

## Mechanic
Super Bowl spot: a brutal American football game. One player is performing terribly — it's Betty White, age 88, getting tackled and talking like someone's grandmother. A teammate hands him a Snickers. He transforms back into a young man. "You're not you when you're hungry." The casting of Betty White was the entire joke and the entire idea.

## Why it worked
Betty White was the perfect embodiment of the wrong-person-in-the-wrong-context insight. The contrast between her actual identity (beloved elderly comedienne) and the expected identity (young male athlete) was so extreme that no explanation was needed. Casting was the creative decision.

## Steal
When an insight is about displacement or mismatch (being the wrong person, in the wrong mood, in the wrong situation), the most efficient execution is extreme casting. Find the human who IS the mismatch.

# Hungerithm (Snickers · 2016)

## Insight
"You're not you when you're hungry" is Snickers' global advertising idea — but it had never been proven in real time. Collective online anger is a measurable proxy for collective hunger; if the internet is furious, people need a Snickers.

## Mechanic
An algorithm monitored Twitter sentiment in Australia in real time — tracking the collective anger level of the internet. The angrier the internet became, the cheaper Snickers got at 7-Eleven: the price dropped up to 82% at peak rage, updating 144 times per day. Users could check the current price at any time.

## Why it worked
Technology as canvas (P18): the algorithm was not a distribution gimmick but a literal instantiation of the brand's advertising idea — it converted the slogan into a working mechanism. User as co-author (P09): every angry tweet became part of the pricing mechanism without requiring direct participation. The idea proved itself by working.

## Steal
Take your brand's core slogan and design a mechanism that executes it in the real world rather than communicates it in media. "Show, don't tell" applied to brand platforms: the idea becomes a product, not a message.

# You're Not You When You're Hungry (Snickers · 2010)

## Insight
Hunger doesn't just make you hungry — it makes you someone else. An inferior, embarrassing, wrong version of yourself. The solution is immediate and simple: a Snickers. The advertising idea maps directly to the product benefit without any mediation.

## Mechanic
An advertising platform built entirely on the displacement mechanic: person A is performing badly or acting out of character, revealed to be hungry, eats a Snickers, returns to being themselves. The variable is who plays person A — Betty White, Robin Williams, Willem Dafoe, a Diva — each casting creating a fresh execution of the same irreducible idea.

## Why it worked
The advertising idea (you become the wrong person when hungry) is infinitely generative: any celebrity miscast in any situation is a new Snickers ad. The idea doesn't exhaust — it compounds. Every new casting is funnier because the audience knows the formula and anticipates the reveal.

## Steal
An advertising idea that can run for 15+ years in 80+ markets must be simple enough to generate infinite variations and specific enough that each variation is immediately recognisable as itself. The Snickers formula: extreme mismatch + one Snickers + return to self. Three components; infinite casting.

# Bouncy Balls (Sony Bravia · 2005)

## Insight
LCD TVs were competing on specifications — resolution, refresh rate, contrast ratio — in ways consumers couldn't meaningfully compare. Sony's Bravia had superior colour reproduction, but "better colour" as a claim was forgettable. The insight: show colour as an experience that is physically, viscerally overwhelming.

## Mechanic
250,000 coloured rubber balls were released at the top of Larkin Street in San Francisco's Tenderloin district and filmed bouncing down the steep hill. No CGI. Real balls, real street, real physics. A four-person soundtrack ("Heartbeats" by José González) and slow motion made the sequence hypnotic. The 60-second and 2-minute versions both circulated; the longer version spread more virally.

## Why it worked
P08 (craft as message): the impossibility of the real-world execution was immediately apparent and impossible to dismiss as special effects. The audience understood instinctively that this was real — the imperfections, the physics, the chaos. That physical reality transferred to the product claim: this TV shows real colour, not processed colour. P11 (absurdism): the premise — 250,000 balls down a San Francisco hill — is preposterous in scale, which is precisely what made it magnetic. The spot generated enormous earned media before "going viral" was a mainstream concept.

## Steal
When a product attribute is abstract (colour quality, sound quality, precision), find the physical analogy that makes it impossible to ignore. The physical analogy must be impossible to replicate with a smaller budget — scale and physical reality are what make the transfer of quality credible.

# Paint (Sony Bravia · 2006)

## Insight
Following Bouncy Balls, Sony needed to sustain the "colour is a physical experience" territory without repeating the mechanic. The brief was identical but the execution had to escalate — more dramatic, more visceral, more impossible to dismiss.

## Mechanic
A decommissioned tower block in Glasgow was used as a canvas. Seventy thousand litres of paint were discharged via pneumatic cannons, squibs, and mortars — exploding across the facades of the building in slow motion. Real pyrotechnics, real paint, filmed over ten days and compressed into a 90-second spot. Set to Rossini's "The Thieving Magpie." No CGI.

## Why it worked
The Balls campaign established the formula: physical reality + extraordinary scale + colour = Bravia. Paint escalated each variable. The use of an abandoned building added the implicit message of transformation — grey concrete converted to colour spectacle, which is exactly what a Bravia TV does to your living room. The operatic score (Rossini) gave the destruction a formal beauty that prevented it from reading as chaotic or destructive. P08 craft: the production logistics were legible — audiences understood this was real, expensive, and one-shot. That understanding amplified the brand's quality signal.

## Steal
When a campaign has established a physical mechanic, the second execution must escalate the physical stakes rather than repeat the format. Escalation signals that the brand has more to say; repetition signals that the first idea was the only idea.
