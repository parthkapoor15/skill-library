# Shade Running (Nike · 2012)

## Insight
Running in extreme heat is a challenge most runners face but no sports brand addresses directly. The adaptation strategies runners develop — hunting shade, running at dawn — are evidence of genuine athletic commitment overlooked by aspirational advertising.

## Mechanic
A film celebrating the specific, unglamorous tactics of runners adapting to heat — running through shade patches, choosing routes by cover, starting before sunrise. The ordinary difficulty of real runners is treated as athletic achievement.

## Why it worked
By focusing on a specific, real running challenge rather than an aspirational race or record, the film earns trust with actual runners while communicating Nike's understanding of their lived experience. Specificity is empathy.

## Steal
Find the most specific, unglamorous version of your audience's engagement with the product. Celebrate that, not the aspirational version. The specific truth is always more credible than the aspirational lie.

# Swoosh Vote (Nike · 2018)

## Insight
Young Black Americans feel their civic power is routinely diminished — but sport has always been a space where they set the terms. Nike owns that space; channelling it into voter engagement is the only non-hypocritical call to action the brand can make.

## Mechanic
Nike turned the Swoosh into a voting symbol during the 2018 US midterms — integrated campaign with athlete-led content, social amplification, and a direct voter-registration mechanic. Users could interact and share pledges tied to the brand's Equality platform.

## Why it worked
The Swoosh is already a cultural badge of identity for the demographic Nike was reaching. Attaching voter registration to that badge made the ask feel native, not corporate. P09 (user as co-author) scaled reach through personal sharing; P04 (brand as activist) gave it credibility by linking to Nike's existing Equality work, not arriving from nowhere.

## Steal
If your brand has a pre-existing cultural symbol with genuine meaning for an audience, you can load that symbol with new civic weight without explaining yourself. The symbol does the persuading; you only need to point.

# Tag (Nike · 2001)

## Mechanic

A TV film: a game of tag breaks out among adults in a city — business people, commuters, pedestrians — suddenly transformed into the completely absorbed seriousness of children playing. The game escalates through an entire urban landscape. The production scale (hundreds of participants, multiple city blocks) is matched by the sincerity of the participants' commitment to the game.

## Insight

The desire to play never leaves us — adults suppress it, but the moment permission exists, it erupts with full childhood intensity. Nike's "Just Do It" platform has always been about releasing the athlete (or child) inside the adult — Tag is the most literal, joyful expression of that idea.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): adults playing tag with absolute seriousness is inherently comic, but also genuinely moving — the viewer simultaneously laughs and feels the longing the film describes. P16 (long-form drama) gave the game time to escalate from one person to a citywide phenomenon, making the emotional payoff proportionate to the scale. The film requires no product message — the feeling it creates is the brand message.

## Steal

If a brand's emotional territory is about releasing joy or ability that's been suppressed, show the release rather than describing what's been suppressed. Find the simplest, most universal game and play it straight.

# The Cage (Nike · 2002)

## Insight
Before the World Cup, football's most authentic form is the cage game — street football played in a confined space where every player's individual skill is exposed. The world's best players all came from somewhere small. The cage is where greatness begins, before systems and teams.

## Mechanic
A long-form film featuring the world's top footballers competing in a cage tournament — one-on-one or small group, high intensity, pure individual skill. The format of street football applied to elite players, stripping away team context.

## Why it worked
The cage game makes elite players' individual skill visible in a way that match footage cannot. It also connects football's global elite back to their origins in informal settings, which resonates with the sport's fan base worldwide. Nike owns street football culture by dramatizing it.

## Steal
For team sports, find the individual version of the game that reveals the player's personal identity rather than their system role. Individual excellence in a collective sport is rarely shown — that gap is brand territory.

# The Jogger (Nike · 2012)

## Insight
Athletic achievement in Nike advertising typically features elite bodies. But the majority of Nike customers are not elite athletes — they are people fighting inertia. The most honest version of "Just Do It" is not a superhero moment but a person forcing themselves to run when everything resists.

## Mechanic
A short film following a non-elite runner — an overweight boy — on a long run through suburban America. No Nike athletes. No speed. Just sustained effort in the face of difficulty. The running is the achievement.

## Why it worked
The spot redefines the Nike athlete from the outside in: anyone who moves is an athlete. The child's physical reality is treated with the same cinematic respect Nike gives to Olympians. The emotional impact comes from recognizing that the difficulty shown is real and the courage required is genuine.

## Steal
The most radical thing an athletic brand can do is define athletic achievement at the human scale rather than the elite scale. The runner who barely makes it is a more powerful Nike ambassador than the one who wins — because more people recognize themselves in struggle than in triumph.
