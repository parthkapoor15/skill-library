# Alexander Hamilton (Got Milk · 1993)

## Insight
Deprivation is funnier when the stakes are highest. An Alexander Burr history obsessive with $10,000 riding on a phone-in trivia answer has the highest imaginable stakes for a man with peanut butter in his mouth and no milk in his refrigerator. The comedy of the spot comes from perfect proportionality: massive stakes, tiny physical obstacle, no milk.

## Mechanic
The original and most famous Got Milk? execution. A man alone in his house is eating peanut butter sandwiches while surrounded by Aaron Burr memorabilia. He wins a trivia call-in radio contest asking "who shot Alexander Hamilton?" — but cannot answer through the peanut butter. The radio host hangs up. He staggers to his empty refrigerator. Tagline: "Got milk?" 60-second TV spot. Created the Got Milk? campaign and the tagline used for over 20 years.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): the proportionality is the joke — the world's most specialized knowledge rendered useless by the world's most mundane problem. The film is perfectly constructed: the backstory (why he knows the answer), the escalation (the stakes of the call), and the deflation (the empty fridge) are all necessary. Remove any element and the spot fails. This execution is genuinely near-perfect in its construction.

## Steal
Absurdist comedy requires precision of cause and effect. The peanut butter and the trivia question are not arbitrary — they create the exact conditions under which milk's absence is maximally consequential. Identify the scenario where your product's absence has the most embarrassing or costly consequences for the most sympathetic person.

# Heaven or Hell (Got Milk · 1994)

## Insight
The deprivation premise can be extended to any universe — even the afterlife. If the worst thing that can happen to you is running out of milk, the logical endpoint is an afterlife defined by milk abundance or milk absence.

## Mechanic
A spot imagining the afterlife as determined by milk availability. A man arrives somewhere that appears to be heaven — clouds, peacefulness, an abundance of cookies, brownies, and chocolate cake. But no milk. The horror dawns. He is not in heaven. Tagline: "Got milk?" A follow-on execution to the Alexander Hamilton original, extending the deprivation logic to cosmic scale.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): scaling the deprivation premise to the afterlife is a natural escalation of the Got Milk? logic — if milk's absence is tragic in life, imagine eternity without it. The spot's effectiveness depends on the previous spot having established the deprivation insight; it rewards an audience already familiar with the campaign's mode.

## Steal
When a campaign premise is genuinely strong, it can be extended to increasingly extreme scenarios without weakening the underlying insight. Each escalation should discover a new dimension of the insight rather than merely repeating the original mechanic in a new setting.

# Made of Black (Guinness · 2015)

## Insight
Guinness's defining visual characteristic — its darkness — was never meaningfully connected to its African and Black diaspora audience in a way that felt owned by them rather than assigned to them.

## Mechanic
A cinematic manifesto film celebrating Black youth culture, creativity, and identity across Nigeria and Africa, using Guinness's blackness as a metaphor for the depth, complexity, and strength of the audience's own identity. Long-form, high production, culturally embedded.

## Why it worked
Truth-telling (P13): articulating an identity pride with specificity to an audience that had not seen itself reflected in the brand at this register. Long-form drama (P16): the scope of the film gave the identity claim room to feel earned rather than sloganeered. The product metaphor (black = depth, character) connected brand attribute to human attribute without feeling forced.

## Steal
When a product's most obvious visual or physical characteristic can be reframed as a metaphor for the audience's own self-image, the brand attribute becomes an identity statement rather than a product fact.

# Noitulove (Guinness · 2003)

## Insight
Guinness's depth — its darkness, its complexity, its unhurried pour — connects it to something ancient, not fashionable. Running evolution backwards reveals that patience and depth are primordial, not modern, virtues.

## Mechanic
A film running human evolution in reverse — from modern bar to prehistoric sea creatures — scored to an original composition. The final shot: a pint of Guinness being poured. "Guinness. Good things come to those who wait." Three minutes of reverse time as proof of the brand's central claim.

## Why it worked
The reverse-time structure was not a trick but an argument: good things — Guinness, human complexity — took a long time to develop and are worth the wait. The craft of the reverse animation gave the claim physical weight. You don't argue with three minutes of evidence.

## Steal
When your brand benefit is patience or depth, dramatise the time investment rather than the reward. Show the cost of great things, not just the greatness. The audience respects what took effort.

# Sapeurs (Guinness · 2014)

## Insight
Dignity and style are not luxuries — they are choices that can be made in any circumstance. The Sapeurs of the Congo, who dress in immaculate European finery despite extreme poverty, are the most literal proof of human autonomy through aesthetics.

## Mechanic
A documentary-style film following La Sape — the society of Congolese men who devote their resources to extraordinary fashion as a form of identity and resistance. No product placement beyond the brand's association with their world. Pure documentary craft in service of a brand claim: "Made of more."

## Why it worked
Guinness didn't find a spokesperson — it found a philosophy that already existed and was more powerful than any brand could invent. The Sapeurs made the point (inner character expressed outwardly, regardless of circumstance) better than any script. The brand's restraint — staying out of the way — was the creative decision.

## Steal
The most credible brand stories already exist in the world. Your job is to find them, not invent them. A real human story told with documentary restraint is more persuasive than the best crafted fiction.
