# Happiness Factory (Coca-Cola · 2006)

## Insight
A vending machine is a mundane transaction. A coin enters; a can exits. But Coca-Cola's emotional promise is happiness — something that cannot exist in a machine. What if the transaction was actually magic and we just couldn't see it?

## Mechanic
An animated film showing what happens inside a Coca-Cola vending machine: an entire ecosystem of tiny creatures, each with a specific role in the journey of a coin to a Coke — love, adventure, sacrifice, craft — all dedicated to delivering one moment of happiness to the person outside.

## Why it worked
The vending machine is the least magical context for a brand that sells magic. By inverting the mundane exterior to reveal an extraordinary interior, the film makes the emotional promise literal without losing its fantastical nature. The animation quality is itself an argument: this much craft went into the film just as this much invisible care goes into the drink.

## Steal
Find the product's most mundane moment — the moment farthest from the brand promise — and reveal what is supposedly happening "inside" it. The container's ordinariness makes the revelation of extraordinary content disproportionately powerful.

# Heist (Coca-Cola · 2010)

## Insight
A Coke is a small pleasure, but it's worth going to some trouble for. The slight inconvenience of wanting something badly enough to take a risk for it is actually a compliment to the product's desirability.

## Mechanic
An absurdist film spot structured as a heist narrative — elaborate planning, multiple actors, escalating stakes — for the ultimate goal of obtaining a Coca-Cola. The disproportionate effort for a soft drink is played completely straight.

## Why it worked
The heist genre's conventions (cool, competent criminals, intricate plans) applied to a trivial object creates immediate comedy. The implicit argument is that Coke is worth the effort — a product aspiration statement delivered through absurdist inversion rather than direct claim.

## Steal
Borrow genre conventions from film that imply a level of desire or value your product doesn't normally warrant, then play the conventions completely straight. The gap between the genre's emotional weight and the product's actual stakes generates the brand truth.

# Hilltop (Coca-Cola · 1971)

## Insight

In 1971, America was fractured by Vietnam, race, and generation. Coca-Cola wanted to say something about peace and unity — but every brand trying that looked cynical. The insight: don't make a statement about the world, make a picture of the world you wish existed, and put it on a hill in Italy with real young people from every nation singing together.

## Mechanic

A TV film directed by Harvey Gabor: a multicultural group of young people on a hilltop in Manziana, Italy, singing "I'd Like to Buy the World a Coke" (written by Bill Backer at McCann). 200 young people from 30+ countries. Shot in Europe; the song became a hit when released as a standalone record. Coca-Cola received 100,000 letters asking where they could buy the song. It was re-released as a pop single by The New Seekers.

## Why it worked

P07 (invisible brand): the brand appears in the jingle and a final product shot, but 95% of the film is a vision of human harmony that exists independently of the product. The audience doesn't feel sold to — they feel invited. P16 (long-form drama) gave the song time to build into a genuine emotional experience. P10 (serialization and ritual): "Hilltop" established a template for Coke as the brand of moments of universal human connection, a territory occupied for decades afterward.

## Steal

In a divided cultural moment, the brand that pictures unity rather than comments on it earns the emotional territory. Don't address the conflict — embody its resolution. The image must be vivid enough that people want to live in it.

# It's Mine (Coca-Cola · 2008)

## Insight
A Coke balloon floating through a crowd becomes a shared object of desire — everyone wants it, but that wanting is playful rather than competitive. The product unites the crowd even as individuals chase it.

## Mechanic
An animated Super Bowl spot featuring a giant Coca-Cola balloon floating through a parade, with various characters — including Stewie and Charlie Brown from rival network productions — pursuing it. The chase is festive rather than aggressive.

## Why it worked
The animated format removes brand category risk while delivering charm at scale. Featuring characters from competing entertainment properties (a cheeky stunt) generates cross-brand attention. The balloon as shared object of desire is a visual metaphor for the product's universal appeal without stating it.

## Steal
When your product has genuine universal desirability, visualize that desirability as a physical object that everyone pursues — not competitively, but joyfully. The chase should feel like play, not conflict. The shared wanting is the brand truth.

# Masterpiece (Coca-Cola · 2023)

## Insight
Great art and a cold Coca-Cola share the same quality — the power to transport you from the mundane present into a moment of pure feeling.

## Mechanic
A film where a student in an art gallery sees artworks come to life and a Coca-Cola bottle pass between famous paintings — Van Gogh, Warhol, Modigliani, Hokusai — each rendered in the artist's authentic style through a combination of live action and animation.

## Why it worked
The craft itself made the argument: a brand that can commission this quality of animation and art direction earns its association with masterpiece-level experience. The product barely appears; the feeling does all the work.

## Steal
Position your brand alongside the highest-status cultural references without claiming to be equivalent. The juxtaposition transfers prestige; the craft proves the brand belongs in the conversation.
