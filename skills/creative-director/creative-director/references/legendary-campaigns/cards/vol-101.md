# Skinhead (The Guardian · 1986)

## Insight

Newspapers — and journalism itself — are defined by the incompleteness of any single perspective. A scene understood from one angle is dangerous; the same scene understood from all available angles is truth. The Guardian was making an argument about open journalism before that phrase existed.

## Mechanic

A 30-second TV film: from one angle, a skinhead is seen running toward a businessman — apparently an attack. Cut to a wider angle: the skinhead is actually running toward the man to push him out of the way of a falling load of bricks from a building. The tagline: "An event seen from one point of view gives one impression. Seen from another point of view it gives quite a different impression. It's only when you get the full picture you can fully understand what's going on." The Guardian.

## Why it worked

P17 (design as idea): the film's structure is its message — the reveal is not a twist but a demonstration of the principle being argued. The form enacts the content. P13 (truth telling): the argument about media bias and perspective was real and uncomfortable, and The Guardian stated it directly. The three-act structure (threat → rescue → revelation) compressed a complex epistemological argument into 30 seconds without oversimplifying it.

## Steal

When your brand's core argument is about perspective or understanding, build the execution so that the audience experiences the argument rather than hearing it stated. Let the structure of the communication be the proof of the claim.

# Three Little Pigs (The Guardian · 2012)

## Insight
In the age of social media, a single news event is no longer a fact — it is a conversation where every participant has a different version. The Guardian's "open journalism" editorial stance needed a demonstration, not a description. The challenge: how do you make an editorial philosophy feel urgent and necessary rather than abstract?

## Mechanic
A two-minute film retells the Three Little Pigs fairy tale as a breaking news story, showing how the narrative evolves as social media, police, forensics, financial journalism, and protest movements all generate competing versions. Each platform layer adds a contradictory truth. The film ends with the moral implicit: The Guardian's open journalism is the only way to navigate this complexity. No voiceover. The story does the arguing.

## Why it worked
The fairy tale — a story everyone knows — provided instant legibility. The social media overlay was 2012's lived reality. The long-form format (P16) was essential: the complexity of the narrative could not be compressed without losing the argument. The truth-telling pattern (P13) was The Guardian's actual editorial product, not a campaign conceit. The film demonstrated the brand's mission through narrative rather than asserting it through a tagline. Cannes recognized it as a genuinely new form of brand film.

## Steal
When your brand's core value is a process (open journalism, scientific rigor, transparency), dramatize the process as a story — don't name it. Let the audience experience what your brand does, not what it says it does.

# JFK Unsilenced (The Times · 2019)

## Insight
JFK's 1963 Dallas speech was never delivered — he was assassinated before reaching the venue. For decades, journalism has documented what was lost. The Times had an opportunity to demonstrate what responsible media does that no one else can: complete the record.

## Mechanic
Using AI voice synthesis trained on JFK's existing speech patterns and recordings, Rothco reconstructed the undelivered Dallas Trade Mart speech and played it at the exact venue, 55 years later. The restored speech was broadcast and published by The Times. Tech was the journalism — not a gimmick, but the act of reporting itself.

## Why it worked
The technology (P18) did not exist to create novelty; it was deployed to complete a factual record that mattered. Truth-telling (P13) was the editorial mission — the AI was simply the most rigorous available instrument. For a news brand under pressure about media integrity, this demonstrated that quality journalism uses whatever tools best serve accuracy.

## Steal
When your product claim is "truth" or "quality," use the most demanding available technology to demonstrate that claim through an act — not an assertion. Let the difficulty of the task validate the brand's commitment.

# Trillion Dollar Campaign (The Zimbabwean · 2008)

## Insight
Zimbabwe's hyperinflation had rendered its currency literally worthless — $100 trillion notes existed but could not buy bread. The Zimbabwean newspaper, exiled in South Africa and banned in Zimbabwe, faced crippling import taxes designed to silence it. The insight: the evidence of the Mugabe government's economic destruction was physical — you could hold it in your hand.

## Mechanic
The agency printed outdoor posters and newspaper ads using genuine Zimbabwean billion- and trillion-dollar banknotes as the paper and ink. The banknotes cost less than the paper they'd be printed on if purchased conventionally. A billboard that was literally made of money — worthless money — with the headline "It's cheaper to print this on money than paper." P17 (design as idea): the medium was the entire argument. P13 (truth-telling): the economic reality of hyperinflation was made physically undeniable. P04 (brand as activist): a newspaper fighting for its survival used its own oppression as the story.

## Why it worked
There is no separation between the idea and the execution: the Zimbabwean banknotes were not a reference to hyperinflation, they were hyperinflation. The campaign cost nothing because the currency was worthless. The international media attention generated more press coverage than the newspaper could have purchased. Cannes Grand Prix and D&AD Black Pencil recognized a genuinely historic creative solution — using the problem as the medium. One of the most cited examples in advertising history of design doing the full argument.

## Steal
When the evidence of an injustice is physical, use the physical evidence as the medium. Don't describe the problem — embody it. The most powerful proof of an argument is when the argument is made of the problem itself.
