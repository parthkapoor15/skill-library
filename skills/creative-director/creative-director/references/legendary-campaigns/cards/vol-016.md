# The Proud Whopper (Burger King · 2014)

## Insight
The San Francisco Pride Parade draws hundreds of thousands of people. Burger King operates in the neighbourhood. What if the brand's support for Pride wasn't a campaign but a product — and a moment of personal discovery?

## Mechanic
During SF Pride 2014, Burger King sold a "Proud Whopper" — a rainbow-wrapped Whopper with a message inside the wrapper: "We are all the same inside." The burger was identical to a regular Whopper. Customers only discovered this when they opened the wrapper.

## Why it worked
The product IS the message — the reveal (same burger inside) enacts the campaign's truth in the customer's hands. P04 (Brand as Activist) through product action rather than statement. P09 (User as Co-Author): every customer who unwraps and reads is participating in the experiment. The PR is the product experience.

## Steal
When your brand wants to make a values statement, make it through the product rather than around it. The physical encounter with the product truth is always more credible than the surrounding campaign.

# Stevenage (Burger King · 2020)

## Insight
Football video games allow any team to be sponsored by any brand. Stevenage FC — the lowest-ranked team in the English Football League — was available in FIFA. Sponsoring the worst real team, then making them famous in the game, is cheaper than any traditional sponsorship and generates more cultural noise.

## Mechanic
Burger King became the main sponsor of Stevenage FC, putting their logo on the team's jerseys in FIFA. Players were then invited to use Stevenage in Career Mode, score goals, and share the footage — earning in-game rewards and real prizes. Stevenage went from obscurity to FIFA's most-played team.

## Why it worked
The enemy is the conventional sports sponsorship (P02): BK chose the opposite of a prestigious club and won anyway. Cultural hijack (P05): inserts into the existing FIFA player community's native behaviour. The budget was a fraction of any Premier League sponsorship. The community did the work.

## Steal
In sports marketing, prestige partnerships are expensive and generic. The least prestigious asset is often the cheapest and most memorable. Own the bottom before anyone notices.

# Subservient Chicken (Burger King · 2004)

## Insight
Burger King's brand promise was "Have It Your Way" — customization, personal choice. But no fast food brand had ever actually given consumers genuine control over brand content. The promise existed as a tagline; it had never been literalized.

## Mechanic
A man in a chicken suit stood in a living room, accessible via a dedicated website. Users typed commands — any commands — and the chicken performed them. Over 400 behaviors were pre-filmed and served based on keyword triggers. The site launched with zero advertising; it spread via email and word of mouth. In the first 24 hours, 1 million people visited. Within a week, the site had logged 14 million sessions.

## Why it worked
It was 2004 — interactive brand experiences online were unheard of at this scale. But the mechanic was not the novelty: the mechanic literalized the brand promise in a way no 30-second spot could. "Have It Your Way" stopped being a claim and became an experience. Users who discovered the chicken didn't just see a Burger King ad — they had a Burger King moment. The viral spread was entirely word-of-mouth because the experience was genuinely amusing and genuinely novel. CP+B built a world-first digital interactive character 13 years before chatbots became standard.

## Steal
When your brand promise contains a verb ("have it your way," "just do it," "think different"), ask: what does this promise look like as an actual mechanic? Not a metaphor in a film — an actual system users can operate. The gap between "claimed" and "experienced" is where brand loyalty is built.

# Turning Their Tweets Against Them (Burger King · 2017)

## Mechanic
Burger King promoted tweets in which customers complained about rivals — specifically Wendy's fans demanding the return of spicy nuggets — essentially using competitor-brand conversation as free media against that competitor.

## Insight
Social media surfaces competitor dissatisfaction in public. Any brand with the nerve to amplify that dissatisfaction gains both reach and credibility — the complaint comes from real users, not the advertiser.

## Why it worked
Enemy or conflict (P02): the adversarial positioning is explicit but the weapon is the competitor's own unhappy customers. User-as-co-author (P09): the complainants did the creative work; BK just amplified and contextualised it. Zero production cost, maximum provocation.

## Steal
Monitor social for authentic competitor dissatisfaction. Promoting real customer complaints against a rival costs almost nothing and reads as more credible than any attack ad. The competitor's weakness becomes your ad budget.

# The Whopper Detour (Burger King · 2018)

## Insight

Burger King has far fewer locations than McDonald's. Almost everyone has been to a McDonald's. What if McDonald's locations were turned into BK's distribution channel?

## Mechanic

BK app: any customer who opened the BK app within 600 feet of a McDonald's location could order a Whopper for 1 cent. The app used geolocation to detect proximity to the competitor. App became #1 in App Store within days. Customers physically visited McDonald's parking lots to trigger the deal, then drove to BK.

## Why it worked

Enemy as canvas meets tech as canvas: geofencing the competitor's location as the activation trigger was structurally brilliant — BK's weakness (fewer locations) became the mechanic. The competitor's infrastructure served BK's campaign. App adoption drove by a factor of 37 during the campaign.

## Steal

Identify the location, moment, or behavior where your competitor is strongest. Design a mechanic that activates there. Using the competitor's dominance as your distribution trigger converts their strength into your reach.
