# Charge (Samsung · 2011)

## Insight
TODO: Verify year. Likely part of Samsung's campaign positioning Galaxy phones against iPhone. Battery life or charging speed as product differentiator — a functional advantage communicated through absurdist humor rather than spec comparison.

## Mechanic
An absurdist spot demonstrating Samsung's charging advantage (or battery life) through an extreme or comedic scenario — likely showing the product advantage in an exaggerated, impossible situation that makes the technical claim memorable.

## Why it worked
P11 + P14: using absurdism to deliver product proof transforms a spec sheet claim into a memorable visual argument. The humor lowers defenses while the demonstration remains credible because the underlying product attribute is real.

## Steal
Technical product advantages are forgettable when delivered as specifications. Embed the technical advantage in an absurd scenario that makes the magnitude of the advantage viscerally clear.

# Flipvertising (Samsung · 2023)

## Insight
iPhone users are algorithmically walled off from Samsung ads — the targeting system is the barrier. What if teaching users to hack the algorithm was itself the campaign?

## Mechanic
Samsung explained publicly how iPhone owners could manipulate their own ad targeting to receive Galaxy Z Flip ads, then enter a sweepstake to win the phone. Retargeting was turned into a participation game.

## Why it worked
The audience became the distribution channel. Algorithm gaming as brand engagement created earned media from the mechanic itself — and put iPhone users through a journey that ended with Samsung content they chose to receive.

## Steal
Make the platform's algorithm or system a visible part of the game the audience plays. When users hack infrastructure on your behalf, the participation signals intent more powerfully than passive ad exposure.

# The Next Big Thing (Samsung Galaxy · 2012)

## Insight
Apple fans queued for hours to buy the iPhone while the new Samsung Galaxy — objectively more feature-rich — went unnoticed. The insight: Apple users are not buying technology; they are buying status and identity. And that identity is vulnerable to being made to look absurd by someone who just walked past them in the queue.

## Mechanic
A series of spots set outside Apple stores during iPhone launch queues. Samsung Galaxy users walk past the queue, using features that iPhone users do not have — NFC sharing, larger screens, better cameras. iPhone queuers notice and become envious or dismissive, defending their wait with increasingly implausible logic. "I'm holding a place for my mom." The humor comes from the queuers' denial. Galaxy users are portrayed as casual, amused, unconcerned with status.

## Why it worked
P02 (enemy or conflict): the enemy is not Apple itself — it is the culture of brand devotion that makes consumers wait outside stores in the cold for a phone. P16 (long-form drama): each spot is a small social comedy with character arcs. The campaign's genius is that it attacked Apple culture, not Apple products, which meant it could not be defended by specifications — only by embarrassment. Widely credited with shifting Samsung's brand position in the US market.

## Steal
When your competitor has cultlike loyalty, attack the cult, not the product. Product comparisons are defensible; cultural critique of brand devotion is not. The iPhone queue is not a product feature — it is a behavior, and behaviors can be made to look ridiculous.

# Ostrich (Samsung · 2017)

## Insight
VR's promise is not escapism — it is the expansion of what's possible. An animal that cannot fly can use VR to experience what it's biologically denied. That's not absurd; that's the truest possible demonstration of what VR is for.

## Mechanic
An ostrich finds a Samsung Gear VR headset and uses it to experience flight. The experience transforms the bird — it begins to run, builds speed, and eventually becomes airborne for real. The film ends with the ostrich actually flying, as if the VR made it possible.

## Why it worked
The absurdist premise (an ostrich using VR) delivers a genuinely emotional message about potential and possibility. The creature's transformation from passive viewer to active flyer mirrors the brand's promise without stating it. The craft is extraordinary; the idea is simple.

## Steal
When your product's benefit is abstract (possibility, potential, freedom), find the most extreme literal embodiment of that benefit in the natural world. Then ask: what happens when you give that creature your product?

# Back-to-School Essentials (Sandy Hook Promise · 2019)

## Insight
"Back to school" is the most relentlessly commercial and optimistic event in American culture — a ritual of new starts, new supplies, bright futures. The same objects children pack in their bags are the objects they use to survive a school shooting.

## Mechanic
A film framed as a cheerful back-to-school supply advertisement — children showing off their new sneakers, their notebooks, their backpacks. The voiceover is warm and retail-friendly. But each object is then demonstrated in its other use: the sneakers for running from a shooter, the socks as a tourniquet, the backpack held up as a shield, the scissors as a weapon of self-defence. It ends with the Sandy Hook Promise hotline.

## Why it worked
Behaviour inversion (P03) at maximum severity: the most positive cultural code (back-to-school) is rewritten with the most horrific context (school shooting survival). The contrast is so violent that the audience cannot process it without experiencing the point physically. Truth-telling (P13): every use demonstrated is real — genuinely what students are taught in active shooter drills.

## Steal
Take the most joyful, commercial, category-coded moment in your cultural calendar and populate it with the darkest possible adjacent reality. The contrast is the argument. The joy makes the darkness more unbearable, not less.
