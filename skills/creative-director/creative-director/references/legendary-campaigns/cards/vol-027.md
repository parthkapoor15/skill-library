# Native Sportscasters (Corona · 2023)

## Insight

Sports broadcasting is entirely in Spanish across Latin America — Indigenous languages, spoken by millions, are absent from the national conversation around the country's most popular sport.

## Mechanic

Corona broadcast official soccer matches with commentary entirely in Indigenous Mexican languages (Nahuatl, Maya, Zapotec) — giving languages spoken by millions of Mexicans their first presence in major sports media.

## Why it worked

Behavior inversion meets absurdism: putting Indigenous language commentary on mainstream sports coverage inverted the invisible assumption that Spanish is the only valid broadcast language. The "absurdity" was actually just justice — making the unfamiliarity the point.

## Steal

Find an exclusion that your category treats as normal — a voice, a language, a community that is simply absent from the conversation. Invert the absence into presence at scale.

# Shampoo (Corona · 2017)

## Insight
Same as Beer/Beach series: Corona's beach aesthetic is strong enough to sustain absurdist parody that reinforces rather than undermines the brand's core sensation — escape, ease, the beach.

## Mechanic
Absurdist film placing Corona's sensory codes — sun, sea, relaxation — in a comedic incongruity with an everyday product category (shampoo), pushing brand imagery into surreal domestic territory.

## Why it worked
Part of the same absurdist series as "Beach," "Karate," and "ShottaSoCo." The series demonstrates that a brand with a fully-owned visual mythology can generate entertainment by displacing that mythology into unexpected contexts. Each film is a standalone execution of the same tension: Corona relaxation vs. mundane reality.

## Steal
A campaign series built from one absurdist engine can scale indefinitely — each entry is a new displacement of the same established mythology into a new incongruous context. The engine is the idea; each film is an execution.

# ShottaSoCo (Corona · 2017)

## Insight
Fourth entry in the Corona absurdist series. The brand's laid-back identity is elastic enough to absorb any incongruous element — here the collision is with Southern hip-hop aesthetics — and remain recognisably Corona.

## Mechanic
Film applying Corona's visual language to Southern hip-hop references, creating a comedic displacement of the brand's beach mythology into rap culture territory. Part of the same absurdist engine as Beach, Shampoo, and Karate.

## Why it worked
The engine remains consistent: Corona sensory world + maximum incongruity = brand-relevant comedy. The hip-hop displacement works because it is culturally legible, the contrast is sharp, and the brand's relaxed confidence makes it charming rather than appropriative.

## Steal
When building a series, vary the incongruent element — the engine stays the same but the displacement target changes each time. This generates novelty within a consistent creative system.

# Dreams of Dali (The Dalí Museum · 2016)

## Insight
Salvador Dalí's paintings depict surreal dreamscapes that are impossible to inhabit. VR makes the impossible inhabitable. The technology and the artist were made for each other — 80 years apart.

## Mechanic
The Dalí Museum created a VR experience placing visitors inside Dalí's painting "Archaeological Reminiscence of Millet's Angelus." Visitors could walk through the landscape, encounter Dalí himself, and explore the impossible geometry of the surrealist world.

## Why it worked
The technology (VR) is not a novelty addition to the art experience — it is the only medium that makes the specific experience (inhabiting a Dalí painting) possible. The match between medium and content is exact. P18 (Tech as Canvas) works here because removing the technology removes the idea entirely.

## Steal
When you have a historical archive or artistic world that cannot be accessed physically, ask which technology would make "inhabiting it" possible rather than "representing it." The difference between representation and inhabitation is the difference between an exhibition and an experience.

# Brewtroleum (DB Export · 2015)

## Insight
DB Export had positioned itself as a "lighter" beer but lacked a differentiating proof. The spent grain from brewing — typically waste — can be converted into biofuel. The byproduct of drinking beer could literally fuel New Zealand.

## Mechanic
DB partnered with Green Biologics to convert spent grain from DB Export brewing into a biofuel blended into regular petrol — Brewtroleum. The fuel was sold at Z Energy stations. For every DB Export bottle sold, a small contribution went toward producing more Brewtroleum. The product was real; the circle was real.

## Why it worked
Absurdism (P11): "Saving the planet by drinking beer" is a fundamentally absurd and hilarious premise. Product as proof (P14): the biofuel actually existed and worked — this wasn't a claim, it was a demonstration of a real closed loop. The combination of absurd premise and genuine proof made the campaign impossible to dismiss.

## Steal
When a brand's waste or byproduct can be converted into something of value, the act of conversion is more powerful than any environmental claim. Real closed loops are better arguments than stated sustainability commitments.
