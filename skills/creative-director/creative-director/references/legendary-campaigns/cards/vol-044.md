# Peanuts (Harley-Davidson · 1998)

## Insight
Harley-Davidson riders self-select out of mainstream consumer culture. The brand had no need to explain itself to non-riders; its job was to reinforce the identity of existing riders and make that identity legible to aspiring ones. The brand's personality was best communicated through attitude, not description.

## Mechanic
A print/OOH campaign using minimalist design — sparse copy, bold typography — to communicate Harley-Davidson's outlaw-adjacent identity through wit and understatement rather than obvious machismo. The "Peanuts" reference possibly involves a David vs. Goliath or "small but mighty" positioning.

## Why it worked
P17 (design as idea): the restraint of Harley-Davidson's print work in this era was itself a statement — the brand was too confident to need elaborate production values. P11 (absurdism): the wit punctured the excessive seriousness of motorcycle advertising, which made the brand seem more self-aware and less earnest than competitors.

## Steal
When a brand's identity is built on anti-establishment values, the communication should mirror those values formally: minimal spend, maximal attitude. Elaborate production values signal mainstream aspiration — the opposite of outlaw credibility.

# Chickens (Harvey Nichols · 2010)

## Insight
Sale periods at luxury retailers create an undignified scramble entirely at odds with the brand's exclusivity positioning — customers behave like panicked animals.

## Mechanic
Outdoor posters showing headless chickens with Harvey Nichols branding for the sale period — a self-deprecating joke about the frenzy the sale creates while maintaining the brand's ironic, self-aware tone.

## Why it worked
Luxury advertising that can laugh at its own sale-period chaos feels more confident than brands that pretend the exclusivity is maintained even during discount. The absurd image is memorable because it is true and unexpected from a prestige retailer.

## Steal
Find the moment your brand's behaviour most visibly contradicts its positioning and beat the audience to the joke. Self-deprecation about real contradictions reads as confidence, not weakness.

# Mannequins (Harvey Nichols · 2011)

## Insight
Harvey Nichols' sale prices are so low they feel shocking even to store mannequins — the discount is dramatic enough to warrant a visible reaction.

## Mechanic
Window display and print showing mannequins in postures of apparent shock or distress at the sale prices being shown — using the physical retail display format as comedy performance.

## Why it worked
Animating inanimate store fixtures as emotional responders to the brand's own sale communicated dramatic discount in an unexpected, memorable visual format. The joke required no copy — the posture told the story.

## Steal
Use fixed, physical brand assets (mannequins, signage, product packaging) as characters responding to your own brand message. Giving inert objects an emotional reaction costs nothing and breaks visual convention.

# Shoplifters (Harvey Nichols · 2013)

## Insight
Luxury shopping is driven by desire so intense it bypasses normal inhibition — Harvey Nichols shoppers would rather steal than go without.

## Mechanic
A film showing people committing increasingly absurd acts of petty crime — pickpocketing, stealing from coworkers, taking from family — then cutting to: they were doing it to fund their Harvey Nichols purchases. "Love it enough to steal it."

## Why it worked
The campaign amplified the brand's aspiration rather than moderating it. Luxury advertising typically flatters; this one made desire appear slightly criminal — which made it feel more real, exclusive, and exciting.

## Steal
Take the uncomfortable implication of intense brand desire — what people would actually do to afford or own it — and make it the premise. Dark desire is more aspirational than polished aspiration.

# Sorry, I Spent It on Myself (Harvey Nichols · 2012)

## Insight
Buying luxury for yourself while giving others cheap gifts for Christmas is something Harvey Nichols shoppers actually do but would never admit publicly.

## Mechanic
A film and product line: Harvey Nichols created actual terrible gift products (a bag of gravel, a roll of sticky tape, a paper clip) sold as gifts, so shoppers could spend their real budget on themselves. Launched with a film depicting the social performance of gift-giving.

## Why it worked
The campaign validated a real behaviour that luxury shoppers already practiced but felt guilty about, turning the guilt into a joke and the product into the punchline. The actual product range made it commercially executable, not just provocative advertising.

## Steal
Identify the guilty, unspoken behaviour your best customers already practice. Name it, celebrate it, and create a product or service that enables it with brand endorsement. The audience rewards honesty about what they actually do.
