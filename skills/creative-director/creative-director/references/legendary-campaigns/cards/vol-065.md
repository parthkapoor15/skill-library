# Fix in Six (Lowe's · 2013)

## Insight
Home improvement feels overwhelming and expensive. Six seconds of genuinely useful information — one clever trick, one real fix — makes it feel achievable. The constraint is the gift.

## Mechanic
Lowe's became one of the first brands to use Vine's 6-second format intentionally as the idea, not just as a channel. Each clip was a complete home improvement tip — stop-motion animation, clear problem/solution structure, zero wasted seconds. The format limitation produced the content quality.

## Why it worked
Most brands on new platforms try to adapt existing content. Lowe's recognised that Vine's six-second constraint was a creative brief, not a restriction. The format forced reduction to pure utility — which is the only thing a home improvement brand can credibly offer in six seconds.

## Steal
When using a constrained format (Vine, Twitter, TikTok 15-second), treat the constraint as the creative brief, not a problem to work around. The best content in constrained formats is designed for the constraint, not despite it.

# Lunchabuilds (Lunchables · 2022)

## Insight
Lunchables is designed to be assembled by kids. The brand truth is constructability — every product is a modular food system. That's also the exact appeal of Minecraft.

## Mechanic
Lunchables partnered with Minecraft to let kids build virtual Lunchabuilds — elaborate Minecraft constructions made entirely of Lunchables food items rendered as blocks. User creations were featured in brand content and playable by others.

## Why it worked
The mechanic is native to both the product (assembly, construction) and the platform (Minecraft block building). UGC generates brand content for free; the Minecraft context provides the audience and the creative framework. Kids are the authors.

## Steal
Find the platform where your audience already spends time doing something that structurally mirrors your product's core mechanic. The crossover is the campaign.

# Man Boobs (MACMA · 2015)

## Insight
Facebook's content policy prohibits showing female nipples but permits male nipples. A breast self-examination tutorial was removed from the platform because it showed breasts. Replacing female breasts with male breasts would make the content platform-compliant while exposing the policy's absurdity.

## Mechanic
MACMA filmed a breast self-examination tutorial using a man's body — specifically a man's chest — to demonstrate the technique. Because the nipples were male, Facebook could not remove the video. The tutorial was medically accurate and fully accessible. It spread globally.

## Why it worked
The absurdist loophole (P11) is the only logical response to an absurd platform policy. The truth-telling (P13) is doubly layered: the medical truth (how to self-examine) and the social truth (content policies have gender-based double standards). The mechanic required no budget and generated global press coverage.

## Steal
When a platform's rules work against your public health or social mission, find the literal loophole. Exploit it completely and publicly. The exploitation IS the argument.

# Find Your Summer (Magnum · 2024)

## Insight
Summer is a feeling, not a season — but most ice cream advertising sells summer as a location or a temperature. People who want to "find their summer" are really seeking permission to indulge in pleasure regardless of external conditions.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign combining cultural hijack moments (P05) with participatory mechanics (P09), inviting audiences to locate and share their personal version of summer pleasure. The campaign travels across platform moments rather than sticking to a single media buy.

## Why it worked
Magnum's brand territory — unapologetic, sensory pleasure — maps cleanly onto the campaign mechanic. User participation naturalizes the brand claim: when people share "their summer," they are also sharing Magnum. P05 gives the campaign cultural legs beyond paid media.

## Steal
Reframe a seasonal product as an emotional state that can be triggered anytime. Then build participation mechanics that let users prove the claim on their own terms.

# Did You Mean Mailchimp? (Mailchimp · 2017)

## Insight
Mailchimp is a brand with an odd name that people often mishear or mispronounce. Rather than suppress this awkwardness or correct it, the campaign celebrates and amplifies it by creating 9 fictional brands with similar-sounding names.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign built around 9 different creative executions — each for a fake brand with a phonetically similar name: MailShrimp, KaleLimp, JailBlimp, MaleCrimp, WailChimp, etc. Each fake brand was developed with full creative execution — film, site, merchandise — before revealing the actual brand. Design-as-idea (P17): the visual identity of each fake brand was fully realised.

## Why it worked
Absurd as carrier (P11): the premise is structurally funny — fully producing nine fake companies to advertise one real company. Design as idea (P17): each fake brand had a distinct and committed visual world. The campaign was more interesting to talk about than most campaigns, and talking about it was advertising. Earned press was enormous.

## Steal
Turn your brand's awkwardness into a campaign. The name people get wrong, the product people mispronounce, the image people misunderstand — these frictions are assets if you lean into them. The internet will talk about a brand that has the courage to laugh at itself.
