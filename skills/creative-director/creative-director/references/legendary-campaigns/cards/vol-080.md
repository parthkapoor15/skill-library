# He Said She Said (The New York Times · 2018)

## Insight
During the height of #MeToo, conflicting testimonies about sexual misconduct were systematically framed as a matter of "he said, she said" — as if two accounts were automatically equal. Journalism's job is to report what it found, not to split the difference.

## Mechanic
A Twitter thread/social post in which The Times restated, side by side, what the accused said and what the evidence showed — demonstrating that the "he said, she said" frame was false when one side was documented and the other was denial. The mechanic is the argument.

## Why it worked
Truth telling (P13): the documentation speaks for itself when presented without editorial framing. The social format gave the argument native context — this was the platform where the "he said, she said" framing was most often deployed. No long form needed; the side-by-side is enough.

## Steal
When the argument can be made through simple juxtaposition of documented claim versus rebuttal, the most elegant format is the side-by-side. Do not editorialise what the evidence says for you.

# Life Needs Truth (The New York Times · 2018)

## Insight
Disinformation degrades more than political discourse — it degrades the shared substrate of reality that community, commerce, science, and daily life depend on. The Times frames truth not as a luxury but as a precondition for life as we know it.

## Mechanic
A campaign arguing that the capacity to agree on facts is foundational to all human endeavor — medicine, parenting, policy, love. Truth is framed not as an abstract virtue but as an operational necessity. Film format conveys the stakes through lived consequences.

## Why it worked
Truth telling (P13): the argument is made through concrete examples of what breaks down when truth is absent — more persuasive than abstract appeals to journalistic values. The framing as necessity rather than ideal makes the ask (subscribe, trust us) feel rational rather than righteous.

## Steal
When arguing for the importance of your category, argue from necessity not from virtue. "Life needs X" is a stronger frame than "X is good." What breaks down when your product disappears is more compelling than what it provides when present.

# The Truth Is Worth It (The New York Times · 2017)

## Insight
In an era of "fake news" and institutional distrust, The New York Times' greatest asset was not its brand name but the specific, verifiable, costly labor that goes into a single piece of investigative journalism. The gap between one true sentence and the investigation behind it is enormous.

## Mechanic
A series of films showing — in painstaking, real-time detail — the cost and process of getting a single fact right: the sources, the verification layers, the legal reviews, the courage required. Tagline: "The truth is worth it." Culminated in a Super Bowl film that listed the steps behind a single ISIS investigation.

## Why it worked
Long-form drama (P16): only extended runtime can show the full weight of what journalism costs. Truth telling (P13): the films do not argue that the Times is trustworthy — they demonstrate the process that makes trustworthiness. In the post-truth moment, process-as-proof was the most credible form of brand defence available.

## Steal
When your brand is under attack for what it does, show how it does it — in granular, time-consuming, unglamorous detail. The process is more credible than the claim. Showing work beats claiming excellence.

# The Truth Is Worth It, Part 2 (The New York Times · 2019)

## Insight
Continuation of the campaign that defines journalism's value through the cost of getting a fact right. Part 2 expands the scope to cover different investigative contexts, reinforcing the platform.

## Mechanic
Subsequent films in the "Truth Is Worth It" series, each following a different investigation — showing the same exhaustive, multi-layered process applied to a new story. The repetition of the mechanic builds the brand's claim through accumulation.

## Why it worked
Long-form drama (P16): each film justifies its length through the density of process shown. Truth telling (P13): the sustained campaign, returning to the same argument through different investigations, builds the case over time rather than in a single burst. Multiple films > one film for a claim this complex.

## Steal
A campaign about process and rigor benefits from being a series — each new execution reinforces the claim rather than simply repeating it. Return to the same mechanic with new evidence rather than searching for a new concept.

# Breaking News (New Zealand Police · 2018)

## Insight
Following the viral success of the recruitment Bollywood video, the NZ Police had established a brand identity built on self-aware absurdism. Maintaining that tone in new executions is both easier (permission established) and harder (can't repeat the same joke).

## Mechanic
A follow-up recruitment film in the same absurdist tradition, presenting police work through a "breaking news" format that subverts the seriousness of news broadcasting. Part of the ongoing NZ Police content strategy established by "Freeze."

## Why it worked
The tonal consistency across executions builds a brand character rather than a one-off stunt. The audience knows what to expect from NZ Police content — and that expectation, combined with the surprise of each new execution, is the ongoing engagement mechanic.

## Steal
When a tonal identity has been established and validated by audience response, build on it rather than proving it again. The second execution should surprise within a known frame, not re-establish the frame.
