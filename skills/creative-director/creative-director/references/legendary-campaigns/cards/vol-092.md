# VS: Undeniable (SickKids Foundation · 2020)

## Insight

Children in hospital are not victims — they are fighters. Charity advertising has trained audiences to feel pity, which creates emotional distance rather than engagement. These kids don't want pity; they want victory.

## Mechanic

Film presenting critically ill children at SickKids Hospital as warriors in a battle — visceral, sports-documentary-style filmmaking, pump-up music, warrior imagery — completely inverting the "sad sick children" charity aesthetic with defiant celebration of their strength.

## Why it worked

Long-form drama meets truth telling: the VS (versus) brand positioning framed disease as the enemy and the child as the combatant — not the victim. The emotional register shifted from tearful sympathy to rallying, energizing defiance. Donors fund warriors, not casualties.

## Steal

Find the most established emotional convention in your category. Ask whether it actually serves the audience it claims to. If not, invert the emotional register entirely. The contrast with category convention is the differentiation.

# Phone It In (Skinny Mobile · 2018)

## Insight
Skinny is the cheapest mobile operator in New Zealand. In the telco category, "cheap" is communicated through comparisons, tariff tables, and discounts — all of which look like desperate value-messaging. What if the cheapness of the product was demonstrated through the cheapness of the advertising itself?

## Mechanic
All advertising was shot on an iPhone in the company's own break room. Employees served as actors. Sound was captured without professional equipment. Every video opened with a title card explaining the production approach: "We made these ads in our break room to keep your bills low." The lo-fi aesthetic was the proof of the promise.

## Why it worked
Behaviour inversion (P03): telecom advertising is among the most expensive and glossy category of communications; Skinny inverted this entirely. Absurdism (P11): the production poverty was extreme enough to be funny rather than cheap-looking. Crucially, the causal logic was stated explicitly — the bad production = your savings — making the absurdism a direct product benefit demonstration.

## Steal
If your brand's core proposition is cost efficiency, demonstrate it through the production choices of the advertising itself. The medium becomes the message. Expensive brands can't imitate this without destroying their positioning.

# Broadway the Rainbow (Skittles · 2019)

## Insight
If Super Bowl ads are performances, what if the brand skipped the ad and staged an actual performance? The most absurd brand decision imaginable is the most Skittles thing imaginable.

## Mechanic
Skittles created a full Broadway musical — a real, ticketed, one-performance-only show — as its Super Bowl activation. The musical was self-aware and strange, examining the absurdity of Super Bowl advertising from inside an even more absurd format.

## Why it worked
The commitment to fully executing the absurd idea (a Broadway show, one night only) generates genuine press, genuine audiences, and genuine earned media. The brand's willingness to spend production budget on something only hundreds of people see directly is itself the statement.

## Steal
The more committed the execution, the more the absurdity reads as confidence rather than desperation. Half-measures collapse absurdist ideas; full commitment makes them iconic.

# Cat Lick (Skittles · 2013)

## Insight
Skittles has no rational brand narrative — its territory is pure sensory absurdism. The brand's permission to be inexplicably weird means any bizarre juxtaposition involving Skittles and desire reads as on-brand.

## Mechanic
A man lets a cat lick Skittles directly from his hand — extended, escalating, increasingly uncomfortable. No explanation offered. Pure deadpan execution of an inherently weird premise. The Skittles "Touch the Rainbow" absurdist universe extended into a new corner.

## Why it worked
Within the Skittles brand universe, the weird is the brand voice. The ad succeeded because it committed to its single absurd premise without apologising or explaining. Skittles viewers have been trained to expect the inexplicable — this delivered on that contract.

## Steal
Brand absurdism only compounds if each execution stays within the established universe of weirdness. The cat lick works because Skittles audiences already have a contract with the inexplicable. New brands cannot import this tone without earning it first.

# Exclusive the Rainbow (Skittles · 2019)

## Insight
Super Bowl advertising is the most expensive, most-watched commercial real estate on earth. What if a brand made an ad only one person could see?

## Mechanic
For Super Bowl 2019, Skittles created "the most exclusive ad ever" — a live commercial performed only for a single viewer, Marcus Crandall, in his home. Everyone else watched a live stream of his reaction rather than the ad itself. The scarcity is the spectacle.

## Why it worked
Anti-advertising as advertising. By refusing to show the ad, Skittles generated more coverage than the ad itself would have earned. The meta-commentary on Super Bowl advertising is itself the brand's irreverent personality made structural.

## Steal
When you can't out-spend the room, out-weird it. The absence of the expected thing (the ad) becomes the story. Scarcity of access can be manufactured by design.
