# Cousins (HBO Go · 2014)

## Insight
Watching HBO content with relatives you barely know creates a specific kind of sustained social agony — particularly during scenes involving sex, violence, or moral complexity.

## Mechanic
A short film in the "Awkward Family Viewing" series depicting the excruciating experience of watching HBO content with estranged or unfamiliar cousins — leading to the pitch for HBO Go as a private viewing solution.

## Why it worked
The comedy works because the situation is hyper-specific (cousins — not parents, not strangers, not close friends) which makes it instantly recognizable to a wide audience. Specificity in social comedy beats generality.

## Steal
In social-situation comedy, the more precisely you name the awkward relationship (not "family" but "cousins who you only see at Christmas"), the more people feel accurately described.

# Faithful (HBO Go · 2014)

## Insight
Watching explicit or morally complex HBO content with someone who holds different values — a religious family member, a conservative partner — creates an acute shared discomfort that neither party can address.

## Mechanic
A short film in the "Awkward Family Viewing" series depicting the specific agony of watching HBO content alongside someone whose beliefs make the content doubly awkward. The resolution: HBO Go, so you never have to share the screen again.

## Why it worked
The scenario targets a specific value-gap discomfort that exists in many real households. By naming a specific audience segment's specific problem (differing values in a shared viewing situation), the product utility becomes immediately legible.

## Steal
Identify the most culturally specific version of your user's problem — not "uncomfortable with others" but "uncomfortable with this specific type of other person for this specific reason." Maximum specificity in problem description makes the solution feel custom-built.

# Voyeur (HBO · 2007)

## Insight
HBO's appeal is not premium TV — it's the feeling of looking into lives more extreme, more honest, and more dramatic than your own. The name of that feeling is voyeurism. Make the metaphor literal.

## Mechanic
A six-story New York building facade illuminated at night with simultaneous projected stories unfolding in different apartments — visible to crowds on the street. An online companion let viewers explore each apartment in detail, revealing how the stories connected. A real-time, participatory narrative that turned passersby into unintended voyeurs.

## Why it worked
The building was not a screen — it was a building with stories inside it, like television made architectural. The crowd on the street became part of the installation. The online extension rewarded deep curiosity. Every HBO show premise (complicated people in private moments) was demonstrated rather than described.

## Steal
When your brand's core value is an experience (voyeurism, discovery, intimacy), build a physical environment that forces the audience to have that experience rather than describing it. The medium is not the message — the experience is.

# Departure Roulette (Heineken · 2013)

## Insight
Most people talk about adventure but plan safety. The fantasy of spontaneous travel — "just going somewhere, anywhere" — is real, but the actual moment of choosing it never arrives because life always offers a reason not to. Heineken's "Open Your World" platform stands for openness; the most literal form of openness is agreeing to not know where you're going.

## Mechanic
A stunt at JFK airport: travelers were asked to spin a roulette wheel that would determine their destination — immediately, with their existing luggage. If they spun it, they had to get on the plane, wherever it was going. The brand gave them money, a Heineken, and filmed what happened.

## Why it worked
The stunt is a genuine test of the brand's stated value. People who accepted were living the "Open Your World" philosophy in real time, not in a film about living it. The resulting footage is more credible than any scripted adventure narrative because the risk was real. The brand put money into a social experiment rather than a production budget.

## Steal
When your brand stands for a value that most people endorse but few practice, create a situation that makes the gap between endorsement and practice visible — and then give people the opportunity to close it. The ones who do become your most credible advocates.

# Legendary Posters (Heineken · 2014)

## Insight
Great moments in sport and culture deserve better than forgetting. Heineken, associated with premium occasions and legendary experiences, has standing to claim ownership of "legendary" — but only if it can demonstrate that claim through something beyond a tagline.

## Mechanic
A series of OOH posters designed to look like legendary moments from sporting or cultural history, rendered in a distinctive graphic style that positions Heineken as the appropriate accompaniment to legendary occasions. The design language is the argument.

## Why it worked
The poster format (P17: Design as Idea) does the work that a film would explain: the visual composition directly associates Heineken with cultural legend without requiring text to make the claim. The invisible brand (P07) technique — minimal product presence, maximum context — signals brand confidence.

## Steal
When you want to own an abstract quality (legend, prestige, greatness), don't claim it in copy. Design a visual system that demonstrates it. Let the aesthetic do the argument that the words would diminish.
