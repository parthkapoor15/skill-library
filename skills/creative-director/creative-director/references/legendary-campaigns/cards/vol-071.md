# When I Grow Up (Monster.com · 1999)

## Insight

Most people end up in jobs they never wanted as children — and they know it. The gap between childhood ambition and adult reality is one of the most quietly painful truths in modern life, universally recognized but rarely acknowledged in public, and certainly never by a recruitment company.

## Mechanic

A Super Bowl TV spot: a series of young children speaking directly to camera about what they want to be when they grow up — but saying things adults actually do rather than childhood dreams. "I want to be a yes-man." "I want to work my way up to middle management." "I want to claw my way to the top." The tagline: "What did you want to be?" Monster.com's URL appears at the end. The spot cost $2 million and aired once — it became one of the most remembered Super Bowl ads ever made and drove Monster's traffic up significantly.

## Why it worked

P13 (truth telling): children saying what adults silently feel is the most efficient truth-delivery mechanism in advertising — innocence makes the uncomfortable comfortable enough to hear. P16 (long-form drama) used repetition of the structure to build cumulative weight — each child's aspirational defeat added to the last. The brief premise (job site needs awareness) was transformed into a cultural truth about work that every adult recognized instantly.

## Steal

When your product solves a pain the audience has normalized, find the most innocent possible voice to state that pain. The contrast between speaker and content creates the emotional impact without the brand needing to argue anything.

# Corazon: Give Your Heart (Montefiore Health System · 2018)

## Insight

Heart disease is the #1 killer, yet people donate organs far less than they could. The emotional distance from death makes organ donation feel abstract — until it becomes personal.

## Mechanic

Long-form film following a heart transplant patient and the donor's family — told through the literal and metaphorical journey of a heart, humanizing the medical procedure into a story of love persisting beyond death.

## Why it worked

Long-form drama meets truth telling: healthcare advertising typically avoids the weight of death. By leaning into grief and gift simultaneously, the film earned genuine emotional resonance rather than manufactured uplift.

## Steal

In a category that systematically avoids its most emotionally charged truths, the brand that tells the real story earns disproportionate trust and attention.

# Puppy Monkey Baby (Mountain Dew · 2016)

## Insight
Mountain Dew Kickstart combines three things in one drink — juice, Dew, caffeine — a product truth that doesn't translate into a conventional benefit ad. The absurdist answer: a hybrid creature combining three beloved things in one body.

## Mechanic
A Super Bowl spot featuring a CGI creature with the head of a pug, the body of a monkey, and the legs of a baby — dancing around and offering cans of Mountain Dew Kickstart to men on a couch. No explanation offered. The tagline: "DEW. JUICE. CAFFEINE." Three things in one.

## Why it worked
Absurdism (P11) at its most committed: the premise was bizarre enough to generate enormous social media commentary ("what did I just watch?") — which was the actual media strategy. The absurd creature was a product metaphor that generated shareability without trying to be relatable. It became a cultural reference point.

## Steal
When a product's differentiator is "combination of three things," a grotesque three-way hybrid creature is a legitimate creative strategy — especially if your audience rewards the weird over the polished.

# If We Made It (Newcastle Brown Ale · 2014)

## Insight
Newcastle had no Super Bowl budget. Rather than ignore the moment, they could participate in it by making "the Super Bowl ad they would have made if they could have." The campaign inverts the genre: instead of an ad, it's a meta-commentary on what an ad would be.

## Mechanic
A series of films showing exactly what Newcastle's Super Bowl commercial would have looked like — complete with all the ridiculous conventions of the genre (sweeping shots, celebrities, slow motion, patriotism) — framed as what "could have been." Anna Kendrick stars, playing herself lamenting the brand couldn't afford her for a real Super Bowl spot.

## Why it worked
Behavior inversion (P03): the campaign performs the Super Bowl genre while critiquing it. The meta-awareness is the hook. Absurd as carrier (P11): the transparency about the advertising machinery is funnier than any joke could be. Limitation as power (P06): budget constraint becomes the entire narrative engine, turning no-budget into the most interesting thing about the campaign.

## Steal
The ad that describes the ad it couldn't make is more interesting than the ad itself. When you cannot buy into a cultural moment, narrate your exclusion from it. Self-aware poverty of means can outperform wealth of production.
