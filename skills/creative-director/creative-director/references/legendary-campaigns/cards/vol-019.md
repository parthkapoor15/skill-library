# The Bear (Canal+ · 2011)

## Insight
Subscribers to a premium TV channel are not buying content — they are buying the experience of people who understand film. The people who work at Canal+ care about cinema the way film students do: obsessively, technically, and with enormous self-awareness about genre and craft.

## Mechanic
A two-minute film shot as a perfectly executed nature documentary. A bear is observed in the wild — eating salmon, playing with cubs. We follow the bear with reverence and curiosity. In the final act, the bear removes a costume. He is a film director. The nature documentary was his bear suit. Canal+'s tagline: "Committed to Cinema." One of the most awarded spots of 2011.

## Why it worked
P16 (long-form drama): the nature documentary format is sustained with absolute commitment — no winking, no breaks in style — until the reveal. P11 (absurd as carrier): the absurdist reveal (the bear is a director) lands harder because the preceding two minutes were played completely straight. It also communicates the brand message with zero claims: Canal+ is a brand made by people who love film so much they will spend two minutes making a technically perfect nature documentary to say so.

## Steal
Commit to the format you are subverting until the last possible moment. The absurd reveal only works when the audience has fully surrendered to the fake premise. Any hint of self-awareness before the reveal deflates the punch.

# Beer Chase (Carlton Draught · 2005)

## Insight
The premise of beer advertising — that the product is worth extraordinary effort to obtain — is inherently absurd when taken seriously. Running an epic action sequence purely for a glass of beer makes the joke visible and the audience complicit in the absurdity.

## Mechanic
A parody of blockbuster action film chase sequences — helicopters, explosions, hundreds of extras — pursued at maximum seriousness for a single end goal: beer. The scale of the production is the punchline. Operatic score underlines that this is a grand, serious epic about nothing more important than a cold drink.

## Why it worked
The film committed completely to its parody premise. No winking at the camera, no breaking of the frame — pure deadpan escalation. The seriousness of the production made the absurd premise funnier. It was also one of the first ads designed to be forwarded via email — pre-YouTube viral, engineered for sharing.

## Steal
Absurdist advertising works when the execution is more serious than the premise deserves. The comedy comes from the gap between scale and subject, not from jokes. Cast the production, not the punchline.

# Big Ad (Carlton Draught · 2005)

## Insight
Beer advertising in Australia had settled into two modes: serious "mates" narratives and lightweight comedy. Neither was self-aware about the conventions of beer advertising itself. The category had accumulated so many conventions — big music, epic visuals, slow motion — that the conventions themselves were ripe for satire.

## Mechanic
An epic, sweeping cinematic commercial — hundreds of men running across a hillside, orchestral music swelling — with a self-referential narration: "This is a big ad. A big, big ad." The ad knowingly advertises the fact that it is a large, expensive advertisement for beer. The final tagline acknowledges the cost: "Be worth it." The joke is the meta-awareness of the ad about itself.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurdism): the absurdism is meta — the commercial's self-awareness of its own conventions is the joke. P03 (behavior inversion): beer advertising conventions (epic scale, slow motion, masculine scenery) are deployed and simultaneously mocked. The Australian deadpan sensibility made the self-reference feel genuinely funny rather than arch or clever. The "Big Ad" spread rapidly in the early YouTube era — before pre-roll advertising existed, audiences shared ads they found genuinely amusing. Cannes recognition validated international legibility of the Australian humor register.

## Steal
Every established advertising category has conventions so embedded they are invisible. Name them. Deploy them sincerely and simultaneously mock them. The meta-awareness of the ad about its own genre is only available to brands confident enough in their identity to not need the conventions to do the work.

# Black Supermarket (Carrefour · 2018)

## Insight
EU regulations banned the sale of hundreds of heritage vegetable varieties — old seeds that had fed Europe for centuries — under standardisation rules ostensibly about consumer safety but practically protecting large agribusiness. Farmers were left with harvests they couldn't legally sell. Carrefour had the shelf space to sell them.

## Mechanic
Carrefour opened pop-up "Black Supermarket" stores selling the banned heritage varieties — tomatoes, potatoes, herbs — under signs reading "We sell what farmers can't." Simultaneously lobbied the EU to change the law. The law changed. Campaign ran across France with media coverage amplifying the activist framing.

## Why it worked
Brand as activist (P04): the action was structural — Carrefour broke EU law deliberately and publicly, using commercial infrastructure as a political act. User as co-author (P09): customers who bought the produce were participants in a lobbying campaign, not just shoppers. The three-part construction (break law + sell product + lobby to change law) gave the story a narrative arc and a victory.

## Steal
Identify a regulation that harms producers or consumers unjustly and use your distribution infrastructure to violate it openly. The most credible brand activism is the kind that risks something real. Combining commercial act + legal challenge + legislative outcome is the gold standard of campaign mechanics.
