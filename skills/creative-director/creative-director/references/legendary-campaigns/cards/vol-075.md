# If You Let Me Play (Nike · 1995)

## Insight

Girls who play sports are statistically less likely to experience teen pregnancy, more likely to finish school, less susceptible to depression, and less likely to tolerate abuse. Sport for girls is not recreation — it is a developmental intervention. The problem: this truth was never stated plainly, in a world where girls were still routinely excluded from sports programs and facilities.

## Mechanic

A TV film: a series of young girls speaking directly to camera, each completing the sentence "If you let me play..." — "I will be 60% less likely to get breast cancer." "I will suffer less depression." "I will be more likely to leave a man who beats me." The girls are matter-of-fact, direct, not pleading. The statistics appear on screen. Nike's swoosh at the end. No athletes, no spectacle, no drama — just children stating facts.

## Why it worked

P13 (truth telling): the data was real, the delivery was direct, and the category convention was to never say any of it. Nike said it in 60 seconds with no softening. P17 (design as idea): the structural simplicity — direct address, one completion per girl, facts only — was its own argument. The restraint communicated more confidence than any production value could have. The film became a touchstone for conversations about girls and sport that are still ongoing.

## Steal

When your brand's social truth can be stated in plain numbers, state it in plain numbers. Statistics are not cold when delivered by the people who live them. Let the facts do the work — trust the audience to feel what the data means.

# Jordan 1, Newton 0 (Nike · 1993)

## Insight

Michael Jordan's hang time and vertical leap were so extraordinary they appeared to literally defy gravity. Isaac Newton's laws of physics were the highest possible authority to invoke — and Jordan had apparently beaten them.

## Mechanic

A print ad with a single line: "Jordan 1, Newton 0." An image of Jordan in the air. The Swoosh. Nothing else. The copy scores a sporting result against the foundational law of physics as if it were a rival team. The joke required no explanation — the audience immediately understood the scoreboard format and the magnitude of the claim.

## Why it worked

P17 (design as idea): the scoreboard format is the entire idea — presenting a scientific constant as a losing opponent. The wit is structural: sports language applied to physics. The economy of the execution (four words, one image) demonstrated creative confidence — no padding, no explanation, no softening. It stands as a masterclass in print economy.

## Steal

Find the highest possible authority figure in your category and position your brand or its hero as having surpassed them. The authority's credibility amplifies the claim rather than undermining it. Make the format do the work: a scoreboard implies competition, victory, and finality in the most compressed way possible.

# Mars Blackmon (Nike Jordan · 1988)

## Insight

Michael Jordan was culturally real in Black America in a way that standard celebrity endorsement could not capture. Spike Lee's Mars Blackmon character (from "She's Gotta Have It") was also culturally real — hyperbolically devoted to Jordan, speaking in the authentic voice of Nike's actual audience rather than aspirational lifestyle copy.

## Mechanic

A TV campaign series: Spike Lee's Mars Blackmon character (obsessed Jordan fan) appears with Michael Jordan. Mars insists that "the secret" to Jordan's abilities is the shoes — Jordan repeatedly denies it. The comedy rests on the absurdity of the claim and on the authentic chemistry between Lee and Jordan. "Is it the shoes?" became a cultural catchphrase. The campaign ran for seven years.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): the premise (shoes as superpower) is transparently absurd, and the campaign made the absurdity the joke rather than trying to sell it straight. P16 (long-form drama): the ongoing dynamic between Mars and Jordan developed across episodes like a sitcom, building genuine audience investment. Nike simultaneously acknowledged and satirized the mythology of the Air Jordan — a rare creative move that made both the brand and the audience more sophisticated.

## Steal

When your product has been mythologized by its own fans, bring the fan's voice into the campaign. Cast the believer, not the athlete. The fan's hyperbole is more believable than the athlete's endorsement because the audience already knows why the fan believes it.

# Maybe It's My Fault (Nike Jordan Brand · 2009)

## Insight
Michael Jordan created an impossible standard of excellence that has defined NBA expectations for two decades. Younger players are constantly measured against him. That comparison is both tribute and burden — and Jordan himself has never publicly addressed it.

## Mechanic
A film in which Michael Jordan speaks directly to camera, breaking his characteristic silence, and takes responsibility for the impossibly high standard he set: "Maybe I should have been more careful about what I made you believe." A confession of accidental damage alongside a manifesto for the next generation's own greatness.

## Why it worked
Jordan's silence was legendary — he almost never gave this kind of direct address. The film is structured as an apology, which is the opposite of what a brand ambassador typically delivers. The unexpected emotional register (regret, responsibility) makes it far more powerful than any celebration of achievement. The twist is that taking responsibility for the standard also affirms that the standard was real.

## Steal
When your greatest asset has become an intimidating burden to your audience, have them acknowledge it directly. The confession of power is itself an act of power. Vulnerability from someone who is never expected to be vulnerable is disproportionately credible.

# Just Do It (Nike · 1988)

## Insight

In 1987, Nike was losing to Reebok — a brand that had captured the aerobics boom. Nike's core was performance athletes; Reebok owned casual consumers. Dan Wieden's insight was not about athletes at all — it was about the moment before you start. The internal negotiation everyone faces before exercise, before any difficult commitment. "Just Do It" is not a brand claim; it is the voice in your head that wins the argument every time.

## Mechanic

The first TV spot featured 80-year-old Walt Stack running across the Golden Gate Bridge every morning — ordinary, unglamorous, undeniable. The tagline "Just Do It" appeared at the end. The campaign ran for decades, encompassing elite athletes, non-athletes, cultural icons, and ordinary people — all animated by the same imperative. It remains active 37 years later. Nike grew from $877 million to $9.2 billion in revenue in the decade following its launch.

## Why it worked

P13 (truth telling): the insight named something universally felt but never articulated by a brand — the moment of resistance before action. P16 (long-form drama) allowed individual films to be genuinely cinematic, earning attention rather than demanding it. P06 (limitation as power): Nike's limitation in 1988 was that it was "only" for serious athletes — "Just Do It" reframed that as the brand's core strength by defining the serious-athlete mindset as something everyone contains.

## Steal

The best taglines name an internal emotional state that the audience already recognizes but has never heard spoken aloud. If the brand can own that phrase — the one that plays in your head at the key moment — the brand lives in a space no media buy can create.
