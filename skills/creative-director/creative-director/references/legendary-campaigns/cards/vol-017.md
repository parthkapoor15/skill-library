# Whopper Freakout (Burger King · 2007)

## Insight
Most people don't realize how attached they are to a product until it's gone. The Whopper had millions of habitual buyers who had never consciously articulated their loyalty. What if you removed the Whopper from the menu without warning?

## Mechanic
BK secretly removed the Whopper from several restaurants for one day and filmed real customer reactions when told it was discontinued. The hidden-camera documentary captured genuine anger, disbelief, and grief. A 30-minute documentary and shortened TV spots aired the reactions. The twist: in some versions, customers are offered substitute burgers — they refuse, or accept grudgingly.

## Why it worked
The experiment revealed a truth BK could never have claimed credibly: people love the Whopper in a way that goes beyond rational preference. The unscripted reactions were proof no ad could manufacture. It showed P14 (product proof) through an emotional demonstration rather than a features demonstration. The absurdism was in the setup — removing a flagship product to prove people want it.

## Steal
When you need to prove loyalty or preference without a claim, remove the product and film the reaction. The absence of a loved product generates authentic emotion that reveals its true place in people's lives. Hidden camera + documentary format lends instant credibility.

# Whopper Neutrality (Burger King · 2018)

## Insight
Net neutrality is an abstract policy concept that most Americans cannot explain despite it directly affecting their daily internet experience. Burger King could translate it into something everyone understands: paying more to get your Whopper faster.

## Mechanic
Burger King filmed real customers being told they would have to pay more for faster Whopper delivery, with a "fast lane" for premium payers and a "slow lane" for standard. The customers' confusion and frustration mirrored exactly what net neutrality repeal would mean for internet access.

## Why it worked
Cultural hijack (P05): attaches to the net neutrality news cycle. Brand as activist (P04): Burger King takes a political position through product demonstration. The social experiment with real customers (genuine reactions) creates credible evidence. The analogy is perfect: the Whopper as internet data, the restaurant as ISP.

## Steal
When a complex policy issue is in the public conversation, ask whether your brand's product or service is a perfect analogy for explaining it. If yes, run the experiment — with real customers, not actors.

# Whopper Sacrifice (Burger King · 2009)

## Insight
Facebook had just introduced the concept of accumulating "friends" as social currency. Everyone claimed their friends list mattered. But did it really? BK identified a cultural tension: people call hundreds of acquaintances "friends" but the word has been devalued. What is a Whopper worth compared to a Facebook friendship?

## Mechanic
A Facebook app: delete 10 Facebook friends, get a coupon for a free Whopper. The app notified the deleted friends that they had been sacrificed for a burger. The social notification was the mechanic — the humiliation of being worth less than one-tenth of a Whopper was the joke. Over 230,000 friends were deleted before Facebook shut the app down for violating privacy policies. The shutdown generated more PR than the campaign itself.

## Why it worked
The idea exposed the hollow currency of Facebook friendship at the exact cultural moment when "friend" was being redefined by the platform. Getting unfriended because of a burger was funny, legible, and human. The app shutdown by Facebook was the second act — BK became the brand that Facebook was afraid of. The absurdism (P11) was structural: the idea that a friendship has a monetary value (1/10 of a Whopper) and can be compared publicly.

## Steal
Find where social norms have created inflated currency (followers, friends, likes) and expose the inflation with a mechanic that puts a price on it. The social discomfort of the exposure is the campaign — not the coupon. Being shut down by a platform is free PR worth more than media spend.

# Whopper Virgins (Burger King · 2008)

## Insight
Every burger comparison ad was made with American consumers who had opinions, brand preferences, and cultural conditioning. What if you gave the Whopper to people who had never heard of McDonald's or Burger King — no brand bias, just taste?

## Mechanic
CP+B sent documentary teams to remote villages in Romania, Thailand, and Greenland — communities where fast food had never reached. Local "Whopper virgins" tried both a Whopper and a Big Mac for the first time. Their reactions were filmed. The Whopper won in unscripted taste tests. A 30-minute documentary and TV spots aired the results.

## Why it worked
The challenge with taste-test advertising is that viewers distrust it — they assume it's staged with brand loyalists or screened results. Going to people who had never heard of either brand removed that objection structurally. The absurdism (flying a film crew to Greenland to feed someone a Whopper) also made the campaign audacious and PR-worthy. The idea was simultaneously product proof (P14) and entertainment through the preposterousness of the premise (P11).

## Steal
When category comparison claims are universally distrusted, remove the variable everyone suspects: brand bias. The more extreme the measure to eliminate it, the more credible the claim. The stunt quality also earns free media that the production budget could never buy directly.

# Gorilla (Cadbury · 2007)

## Insight
Cadbury had a crisis: a salmonella contamination scandal had severely damaged brand trust. Every PR and advertising instinct said: apologize, reassure, demonstrate quality. But Fallon understood that trust is rebuilt emotionally, not rationally. Cadbury's emotional brand territory was joy — pure, irrational, effortless joy.

## Mechanic
A gorilla sits behind a drumkit. Phil Collins' "In the Air Tonight" builds. The gorilla drums the iconic fill with total commitment and visible pleasure. No product, no logo until the final frame. Duration: 90 seconds.

## Why it worked
Three patterns intersect at maximum efficiency. P11 absurdism: a gorilla playing drums is preposterous and inexplicable, which makes it impossible to look away. P16 long-form drama: the entire 90 seconds is structured as tension-build-release, with the drum fill as climax — earned, not given. P07 invisible brand: Cadbury appears only at the end, trusting that the emotional transfer from gorilla-joy to brand is automatic. The spot restored Cadbury's brand likeability faster than any apology campaign could have. The viral spread predated YouTube's advertising capability — it spread because people wanted to share it, not because it was promoted.

## Steal
When a brand needs emotional rehabilitation: do not address the crisis. Address the emotional territory the brand owns. Pure, unexplained joy demonstrates confidence; apology demonstrates guilt. The gorilla didn't say "we're sorry" — it said "we're still Cadbury." The 90-second runtime was essential: shorter would have lost the tension; any copy would have broken the spell.
