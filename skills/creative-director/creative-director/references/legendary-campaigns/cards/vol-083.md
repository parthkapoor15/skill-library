# Help I Want to Save a Life (Organ Donation · 2012)

## Insight
The barrier to organ donation registration is not opposition — it's inertia. Most people who haven't registered simply haven't been given a moment that made it feel urgent and easy simultaneously.

## Mechanic
A social campaign turning everyday platforms into organ donor sign-up moments. Users who declared their intention to register were given shareable content and tools to encourage their network to do the same, creating a chain of commitment rather than a broadcast message.

## Why it worked
Peer-to-peer commitment is more effective than institutional messaging for behavior change. The social layer transformed passive awareness into active social contracts between friends.

## Steal
For behaviour change campaigns, design the commitment moment first — the smallest possible action that creates a social contract. Then build the awareness campaign around that moment, not the other way around.

# Morning After Island (PAE · 2023)

## Insight
In Uruguay, access to emergency contraception was legally restricted in some municipalities. A territorial gap in the law created a situation where geography determined reproductive rights.

## Mechanic
PAE (a reproductive health NGO) created an artificial island in the River Uruguay — positioned in international waters between jurisdictions — where access to emergency contraception was legal. The island was built, publicised, and accessible. Its existence made the law's absurdity concrete.

## Why it worked
The stunt is pure activism executed with design precision. Creating the island is more powerful than any protest, campaign, or petition — it demonstrates the problem's solution while exposing the law's arbitrariness. The story requires no explanation: an island exists because a law doesn't.

## Steal
When a law or rule is arbitrary, find the geographic, legal, or technical edge case that makes its arbitrariness visible by actually occupying it. The existence of your solution is the argument against the problem.

# The Palau Pledge (Palau Legacy Project · 2017)

## Insight

Palau's fragile ecosystem is damaged by tourism, but the government cannot directly tell tourists to behave — that kills the welcome and the income. But children asking adults to protect their home is a different conversation.

## Mechanic

The Palau government embedded a pledge into passport stamps on entry: an eco-promise written from the perspective of Palauan children, asking each visitor to sign it as a condition of entry. No signature, no visa. The government made conservation a legal/administrative requirement — expressed as a moral invitation.

## Why it worked

User as co-author meets brand as activist: the pledge was co-authored by children (their voices, their words) and signed by every tourist — making compliance feel like a gift to children rather than a government rule. Embedding communication in the border control process made it impossible to ignore.

## Steal

Find an obligatory institutional process your audience already goes through. Ask whether the communication in that process serves the most important message. If not, redesign the communication in that moment of capture.

# Never Say No to Panda (Panda Cheese · 2010)

## Insight
Mild peer pressure around food choices is relatable — someone offers you something; you decline politely; life continues. The campaign inverts the social contract entirely: what if refusing food had absurdly disproportionate consequences administered by a silent, inexorable panda?

## Mechanic
A series of spots in which a person declines Panda Cheese in a mundane setting. A giant panda appears, stares at the person, and then destroys something in the immediate environment — smashing a display case, overturning a hospital bed, ruining an office — while maintaining complete silence and an expression of pure neutrality. No explanation. No resolution. Tagline: "Never say no to Panda." Egyptian-produced, became a global viral hit.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): the absurdism works through its commitment to silence and impassivity — the panda never reacts, never explains. The humor comes entirely from the gap between the mundane trigger (declining a snack) and the catastrophic response. Made on a tiny budget by an Egyptian brand and became internationally known, demonstrating that absurdist humor is genuinely culture-neutral when committed to completely.

## Steal
Build a character whose internal logic is consistent even when it defies all external logic. The panda's power comes from its refusal to acknowledge that anything is wrong. The moment the panda explains itself, the joke is dead.

# World Cup Delivery (PedidosYa · 2022)

## Insight
During the World Cup, Argentina's delivery platforms see predictable surge patterns around match times. But what if the delivery app could anticipate exactly when to deliver — before the goal that would cause the surge?

## Mechanic
PedidosYa used real-time match data to predict when goals were most likely and delivered orders to arrive at peak celebration moments. The brand made itself part of the celebration rather than adjacent to it. Orders were promoted as "World Cup delivery" with match-timing guarantees.

## Why it worked
The campaign hijacks the cultural moment of the World Cup (P05) while making customers collaborators in the timing game (P09). The product delivery IS the execution — not a campaign about delivery, but delivery as the event. In Argentina during a World Cup run, no higher emotional context exists.

## Steal
Find the predictable emotional peaks in your audience's calendar year. Design your product delivery to arrive at those peaks — not the advertising about the product, but the product itself.
