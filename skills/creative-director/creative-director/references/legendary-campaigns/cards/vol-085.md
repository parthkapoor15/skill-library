# Michael Jackson (Pepsi · 1984)

## Insight

Michael Jackson in 1984 was the most culturally powerful individual on the planet — "Thriller" had just become the best-selling album in history. His endorsement was not a celebrity deal; it was a cultural merger. Pepsi buying Jackson meant Pepsi buying the zeitgeist.

## Mechanic

Two TV commercials featuring Michael Jackson performing "Billie Jean" and "Thriller"-era music — children mimicking him, then Jackson appearing. Shot at feature-film production standards. The deal was unprecedented: $5 million, artist control of music rights, significant creative input from Jackson himself. During filming, pyrotechnics accidentally set Jackson's hair on fire — the incident became a major news story, generating additional media coverage worth many times the advertising budget.

## Why it worked

P16 (long-form drama): the spots were produced as short films rather than commercials, with genuine cinematic quality that matched Jackson's artistic standards. The campaign demonstrated what happens when a brand secures not just a celebrity's image but their genuine creative participation — the result is qualitatively different from standard endorsement. The incident during filming also provided a case study in crisis communication that reinforced rather than damaged the partnership.

## Steal

When pursuing a major cultural figure for brand partnership, seek genuine creative involvement rather than image licensing. The difference between a celebrity using your product and a celebrity making something with your brand is the difference between an ad and a cultural event.

# Pepsi Generation (Pepsi · 1984)

## Insight

Pepsi had spent years as Coke's challenger — always framing itself against the leader rather than for something. The "Pepsi Generation" strategy inverted this: instead of fighting Coke, claim an entire generation. If Coke is history and tradition, Pepsi is now. Youth culture, music, celebrity — the future belongs to Pepsi because the future belongs to the young.

## Mechanic

A long-running integrated campaign anchored by celebrity partnerships — most famously Michael Jackson (the most expensive endorsement deal of its era, ~$5 million) — music videos, TV spots, and events. The Jackson commercials attracted enormous cultural attention and effectively made Pepsi synonymous with pop culture's hottest moment. The "Choice of a New Generation" tagline framed every execution.

## Why it worked

P10 (serialization and ritual): the campaign created an expectation — each Pepsi generation would have its defining cultural moment. P16 (long-form drama): the Jackson commercials were produced at feature-film quality, blurring the line between advertising and entertainment. The brand strategy was generational identity — Pepsi didn't sell a drink, it sold membership in a moment in time. This proved durable for two decades.

## Steal

Don't fight the category leader on their terms. Instead, claim a territory the leader cannot occupy — in Pepsi's case, the future. If your competitor owns "established," own "what's next." The audience that doesn't want to be defined by the past will follow.

# Your Cheatin' Heart (Pepsi · 1995)

## Insight

Pepsi's cola wars with Coke framed every consumer choice as an act of brand loyalty or brand betrayal. "Cheating" on your cola brand — drinking the rival's product — is the premise. The cultural tension of consumer brand loyalty, treated as romantic infidelity, was ripe for comic exploitation.

## Mechanic

A TV film playing on the cola wars conceit: a Pepsi drinker caught "cheating" with a Coke (or vice versa), framed in the melodramatic language of romantic betrayal. Part of Pepsi's tradition of using the Coke rivalry as comic material rather than serious competitive positioning.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): applying the emotional register of romantic betrayal to beverage choices is inherently comic, and the absurdity highlights rather than diminishes the brand rivalry. P16 (long-form drama) gives the melodrama space to play out. The cola wars gave Pepsi an ongoing source of comic material that required no product claims — the rivalry itself was the content.

## Steal

If your brand has a well-known rivalry, lean into it for comedy rather than positioning. The rivalry generates more attention than any product message, and humor defuses the defensiveness that straight competitive advertising creates.

# Thank You, Mom (P&G · 2012)

## Insight
At the Olympics, the athlete is always the story. But behind every Olympic athlete is a parent — almost always a mother — who drove them to practice at 5am, sacrificed financially, and believed when others didn't. P&G makes products that help run homes. The real P&G hero was already there; no one had framed the story correctly.

## Mechanic
A series of long-form films showing Olympic athletes from their childhood through the games — always framed through the mother's perspective. The athlete's achievement is the backdrop; the mother's journey is the story. "It takes someone who believes in you, so you can believe in yourself."

## Why it worked
P&G didn't claim athletic performance — it claimed the emotional infrastructure of athletic performance. The role of domestic products in enabling that infrastructure is genuine: laundry, household cleaning, personal care. The campaign is not a metaphor; it's a direct claim about what P&G actually does. The emotional power is proportional to the truth of the insight.

## Steal
When the product's role is supporting rather than starring, find the story where supporting is the heroic act. The unglamorous logistics of care — what mothers actually do — are more emotionally powerful than any aspirational narrative precisely because they are real and underappreciated.

# The Look (P&G · 2019)

## Insight
"The Talk" (2017) showed Black parents preparing their children for police encounters. "The Look" showed the other side: the unconscious looks of surveillance, suspicion, and dismissal that Black men encounter in everyday settings — grocery stores, boardrooms, elevators. The sequel insight: the same prejudice that requires The Talk is expressed daily not in words but in glances.

## Mechanic
A two-minute film following a Black man through a series of everyday locations — each one containing a specific "look" from someone who perceives him as a threat, criminal, or lesser. The film is entirely visual: no dialogue, no explanation, no statement. The looks are the evidence. The ending breaks into hope.

## Why it worked
P13 (truth-telling) at its most disciplined: no narration, no brand voice, no explanation — just documentary observation of looks that millions of Black men recognise but that the broader culture rarely sees named. P16 gave it the duration to accumulate weight before breaking toward hope. Companion piece to "The Talk" — together they form P&G's most significant cultural contribution: a brand making racial prejudice visible and legible to audiences who might otherwise not have encountered it.

## Steal
When the truth you need to tell is visible rather than verbal, resist the urge to explain it. Remove narration, remove copy, remove the brand's voice and let the evidence speak. The viewer's recognition of something they have seen but never named is more powerful than any claim.
