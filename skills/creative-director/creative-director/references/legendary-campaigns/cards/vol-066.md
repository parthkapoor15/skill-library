# The Gene Project (Marmite · 2018)

## Insight
Marmite's "love it or hate it" positioning is so strongly held that consumers believe their preference is innate — perhaps even genetic.

## Mechanic
Marmite partnered with a genetics company to create a real DNA test that could indicate predisposition to loving or hating Marmite. Consumers submitted saliva samples; results were delivered with personalised Marmite-branded reports. PR expanded the debate about taste genetics.

## Why it worked
Taking the brand's oldest claim ("love it or hate it") and making it literally, scientifically testable was absurdist at the level of an idea but entirely real as an execution. The product turned a slogan into a biological fact-finding exercise, generating massive earned media.

## Steal
When your brand has a claim that audiences accept as a joke, make it literally, verifiably true. The joke becomes proof, and proof generates media at a fraction of paid advertising cost.

# Lovers, Don't Spread the Hate (Marmite · 2020)

## Insight
Marmite's defining split — love it or hate it — becomes politically charged when ordinary polarisation spills into everyday food opinions. In the culture-war moment of 2020, even spreads become battlegrounds.

## Mechanic
Marmite leans further into its binary brand identity, framing the love/hate divide through the lens of real-world polarisation. Lovers are urged not to convert haters; instead, tolerance and co-existence are celebrated — absurdly, because it's yeast extract.

## Why it worked
The campaign hijacks the cultural moment of extreme polarisation without taking a political stance. The absurdist framing deflects controversy while still generating resonance. Marmite owns the polarisation mechanic so completely that it can comment on society through it.

## Steal
Find the one attribute your brand owns so completely that you can use it as a lens on any cultural moment. When the brand's truth is strong enough, you don't need a new idea — you need a new application.

# Neglect (Marmite · 2013)

## Insight
Jars of Marmite gather dust in the back of kitchen cupboards because the people who buy it (out of love) forget it exists and the people who hate it won't touch it — this neglect is an actual consumer behaviour.

## Mechanic
A film shot in the style of an animal rescue documentary — investigators entering a home to rescue "neglected" jars of Marmite from the back of kitchen cupboards. Played completely straight, with emotional swells and rescue workers treating jars with urgency.

## Why it worked
The parody of charity rescue format is total — no winking at the camera. Absolute commitment to the absurd premise creates the comedy. The insight (people genuinely do neglect their Marmite) is real, making the joke feel true even as the format is ridiculous.

## Steal
Commit fully to the most ridiculous premise your insight supports. Half-committed absurdism reads as confused; total commitment to the absurd logic is the only version that works.

# Rejected Ales (Matilda Bay · 2022)

## Insight
Craft beer enthusiasts have strong opinions — they argue passionately about what beers should exist. What if rejected internal brewery recipes were given to the public to debate and vote on?

## Mechanic
Matilda Bay released beers that had been rejected by their own brewing team, inviting consumers to taste and vote on whether the internal rejection was correct, making the audience the quality-control panel.

## Why it worked
The mechanic gave the most opinionated craft beer audience real power — not performative co-creation but actual influence over what gets made. The absurdity of releasing rejected products created conversation around the quality of the accepted ones.

## Steal
Give your most critical audience access to what you normally hide — the failures, the rejects, the near-misses. Transparency about the editorial process builds more trust than polished presentation alone.

# Follow the Arches (McDonald's · 2018)

## Insight

The McDonald's logo is so universally recognized that you don't need to show the whole thing. The Golden Arches, cropped to a fragment, can function as a directional navigation tool.

## Mechanic

OOH directional signs: the McDonald's logo cropped at different angles to indicate turn direction (left arc = turn left, right arc = turn right, upward arc = straight ahead). No wordmark, no additional information. The fragment navigates.

## Why it worked

Design as idea: only a brand with total visual saturation can strip its logo to an unidentifiable fragment and have it still communicate. The campaign tested brand recognition by betting on it — and won. Confidence is the message.

## Steal

Test your logo at extreme reduction: crop it, rotate it, cut it to 10%. If it still communicates, you have permission to use the fragment as an idea. If it doesn't, you need to build more brand recognition before this works.
