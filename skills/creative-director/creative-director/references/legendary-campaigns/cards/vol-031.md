# The Last Barf Bag (Dramamine · 2023)

## Insight

Airlines have been removing barf bags from seat pockets — but motion sickness hasn't gone away, leaving travelers in a worse position than before. The disappearing barf bag is a culturally recognized, relatable anxiety.

## Mechanic

Dramamine collected the "last barf bags" from airlines that were eliminating them, auctioned them as collectibles, and turned the artifact of motion sickness into a cultural moment — positioning Dramamine as the solution to a problem everyone recognizes but no one talks about.

## Why it worked

Absurdism meets user participation: the barf bag as collectible object inverted its disgusting utility into a conversation starter. By making a taboo subject into an auctionable artifact, Dramamine earned press, social sharing, and genuine product relevance.

## Steal

Find the most embarrassing product in your category. Make that embarrassment the hero by treating it with unexpected seriousness or desirability. Taboo subjects earn attention precisely because no one else is touching them.

# After Hours Athlete (Jameson · 2014)

## Insight
The real sporting arena for many young adults is the bar — the endurance, the strategy, the camaraderie. Celebrating "after hours" performance with the same reverence as athletic achievement reframes drinking as its own kind of sport.

## Mechanic
Long-form film series treating late-night socialising as athletic performance — complete with sports documentary aesthetics, slow-motion, and commentary. Jameson whiskey as the fuel for this alternative athletic universe.

## Why it worked
Sports documentary tropes are instantly legible and emotionally loaded. Applying them to the opposite context (bar nights, not stadiums) creates gentle absurdist comedy while genuinely flattering the target audience's identity.

## Steal
Borrow the visual grammar of a prestigious, emotionally loaded genre and apply it to an everyday, low-stakes context your audience inhabits. The contrast creates warmth without condescension.

# Still Free (Ecko Unlimited · 2006)

## Insight
In a culture obsessed with official gatekeeping and brand control, street credibility is more valuable when it proves the establishment can't stop you. Tagging Air Force One — even via video — said everything about where Ecko stood.

## Mechanic
A viral video depicting someone spray-painting a giant Ecko logo on Air Force One. The footage was made to look authentic enough that the Pentagon was forced to publicly deny it, generating massive earned media. Users spread the video believing it was real; the brand never formally confirmed or denied.

## Why it worked
The denial from the Pentagon became the campaign. Ecko didn't need to buy media — the government's response proved the brand's anti-establishment positioning more credibly than any ad could. Tech-as-canvas (the viral video format of 2006) was used not as a distribution channel but as a credibility mechanism.

## Steal
When your brand's claim is defiance, the most powerful proof is an authority figure trying to shut you down. Design provocations that invite official denial — the denial is the amplification.

# Cat Herders (EDS · 2000)

## Insight
EDS was a B2B IT services company trying to explain complex systems integration to business decision-makers during the first dot-com boom. Its competitors communicated in jargon and abstraction. The insight: what EDS does — managing complex, unruly, independent-minded systems across an enterprise — is exactly like herding cats.

## Mechanic
A Super Bowl spot shot as a western documentary — cattlemen on horseback, sweeping cinematography, dramatic music — herding thousands of cats across an American prairie. Cowboys nurse scratched hands, brush cat hair off their jackets, and stare into the sunset with quiet pride. Tagline: "EDS. Managing the complex. Moving business forward." Played entirely straight, as if cat herding were a noble profession.

## Why it worked
The absurdism was load-bearing strategy, not entertainment: the visual metaphor of cat herding communicated EDS's actual value proposition more clearly than any product description could. Decision-makers who recognized the chaos of enterprise IT immediately understood the analogy. The western film format gave the work dignity and scale — EDS appeared competent and serious even through comedy. Running this in the Super Bowl, then the most expensive advertising slot in the world, signaled to B2B buyers that EDS was a legitimate, substantial company. The meta-message of the spend was as important as the message of the spot.

## Steal
When a B2B value proposition is complex and abstract, find the physical metaphor that makes it immediately visceral. The best B2B metaphors have two qualities: they describe the problem (herding cats = chaos) and the solution (expert herders = EDS manages the chaos) simultaneously.

# Yes Good (Emerald Nuts · 2006)

## Insight
The snack nut category competes on taste claims and packaging. Emerald Nuts found a voice of absurdist humor that made the brand impossible to ignore in a low-interest category.

## Mechanic
A campaign built on surreal acronym humor: each spot decodes the brand name "Emerald Nuts" into a bizarre phrase (e.g. "Evil Marsupials Enjoy Really Awesome Lemon Danishes Nuts Under Trees Swiftly"), playing the comedy completely straight.

## Why it worked
Absurd as carrier (P11): the deadpan delivery amplifies the absurdity. The joke is structural — it repeats reliably, so audience anticipation builds across the campaign. Low-interest category + high-weirdness execution creates memorability through contrast.

## Steal
In a low-interest category, pure absurdist humor with a repeatable structural mechanic (the acronym) builds recognition over time. The formula needn't vary — the new execution per cycle is the fresh element.
