# Mountain (PlayStation · 2003)

## Insight
PlayStation's games allow players to inhabit worlds and situations beyond normal human experience. A campaign that communicates the scale of those possibilities — the epic, the overwhelming, the unreachable — would do more than product features ever could.

## Mechanic
A long-form cinematic film featuring a vast mountain of human figures — potentially thousands of intertwined bodies — as a visual metaphor for the accumulated humanity inside PlayStation's gaming worlds. Epic scale without explanation.

## Why it worked
PlayStation's "Double Life" era established a brand voice of philosophical scale — gaming as a different relationship with reality. Mountain extends that territory through visual spectacle (P16 long-form craft) that communicates the impossible, epic scale of gaming experience without showing a single game. The sublime terror (overwhelming scale + wonder) is the brand emotion.

## Steal
Abstract product experiences (gaming, music, travel) can be communicated through visual scale rather than product representation. If the experience is vast, make the communication vast.

# The First Edible Mascot (Pop-Tarts · 2023)

## Insight
College Bowl games are corporate sponsorship theater — brands put their name on everything but rarely become the event. Pop-Tarts saw that a mascot costume is literally a giant food costume, and that Pop-Tarts are literally edible. The gap between those two facts was the idea.

## Mechanic
At the Pop-Tarts Bowl (college football), the mascot "Strawberry Pop-Tart" was not a promotional character but a sentient pastry who "volunteered" to be eaten after the game. The giant Pop-Tart mascot descended into a giant toaster on the field, was "toasted," and then physically served to players and fans. Entirely absurd, entirely real.

## Why it worked
Three patterns stacked: P05 (hijacking the cultural moment of college football), P09 (crowd participation and the feeding ritual), P11 (complete commitment to absurdist logic — the mascot was actually eaten). The internet responded because the stunt had internal consistency: if you name a Bowl game after a food and put a food mascot in it, follow the logic to its absurd conclusion. The willingness to commit made it shareable and unforgettable.

## Steal
When a brand has naming rights or sponsorship of an event, don't just put your logo on it — inhabit the event through the product's own logic. Follow the absurd premise to its inevitable, committed conclusion.

# The Rose That Grew From Concrete (Powerade · 2014)

## Insight
Sports drinks advertise to champions, but the actual lived experience of athletic aspiration is overcoming circumstances that shouldn't produce champions. The athletes who fight hardest are rarely the ones shown in the advertising.

## Mechanic
A long-form film drawing on Tupac Shakur's poem "The Rose That Grew From Concrete" as a framework for stories of athletes who defied socioeconomic or physical adversity to compete at high levels. The Powerade brand is positioned as a supporter of overlooked determination.

## Why it worked
The Tupac poem provides cultural legitimacy and emotional depth outside the conventional sports advertising grammar. By centering adversity rather than achievement, Powerade found a human truth that Nike and Gatorade's performance-first messaging left unclaimed.

## Steal
In a category where everyone shows the finish line, own the race. Find the human moment before achievement — the specific adversity, the specific doubt — and make that the campaign. Achievement without context is just a highlight reel.

# Dr. Rick (Progressive · 2021)

## Insight
People become their parents. It's inevitable, universal, and secretly terrifying — especially when it comes to homeowner habits. Insurance exists at exactly the life stage when this transformation happens.

## Mechanic
Dr. Rick, a "parentamorphosis" specialist, helps new homeowners avoid turning into their parents through absurd therapeutic interventions. The recurring character runs across a long campaign of spots treating the joke seriously.

## Why it worked
The serialised character creates appointment viewing in a category (insurance) that generates zero organic interest. The insight (becoming your parents) is universally recognised without being mean. The absurdist therapeutic framing keeps the execution fresh across many executions.

## Steal
Find the universal human anxiety that coincides exactly with your product's moment of purchase. Build a recurring character around it. The ritual of recognising yourself in the joke IS the brand relationship.

# Pinocchio (Progressive · 2010)

## Insight
Insurance customers suspect they're being lied to constantly. The absurdist move: what if the spokesperson's nose actually grew with every misleading claim, making the industry's dishonesty physically visible?

## Mechanic
A film playing on the Pinocchio fairy-tale trope applied to insurance industry claims — visible, literal lying made visual and absurd, with the honest alternative offered as contrast.

## Why it worked
Using a universally understood symbol of lying (the growing nose) to satirise the insurance industry's communication habits positioned Progressive as the honest alternative without making a direct competitive attack. The absurdity was the credibility mechanism.

## Steal
Category conventions that involve implicit dishonesty (price anchoring, hidden fees, fine print) can be exposed through fairy-tale or folk-tale archetypes without legal risk. Pinocchio is recognisable shorthand that doesn't require naming a competitor.
