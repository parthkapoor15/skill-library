# A Boy and His Atom (IBM · 2013)

## Insight
IBM's researchers work at the atomic scale — a realm invisible to human perception. Making that scale tangible requires a medium that operates at that scale. The technology is the canvas that no other brand could use.

## Mechanic
IBM scientists used a scanning tunneling microscope to move individual atoms, frame by frame, to create the world's smallest stop-motion film — a boy playing with a single carbon monoxide atom. Certified by Guinness World Records as the smallest film ever made.

## Why it worked
The technology (atomic manipulation) is the craft and the message simultaneously. P08 (Craft as Message): the execution at the atomic scale demonstrates IBM's research capability better than any claim. P18 (Tech as Canvas): the STM is not just used by IBM — it IS IBM's work, made visible for the first time.

## Steal
When your organisation works at a scale that most people cannot perceive, find a way to make that scale visible without simplifying it. The awe of the actual is more effective than any metaphor.

# Smarter Cities (IBM · 2013)

## Insight
IBM builds technology infrastructure for cities. Abstract corporate claims about "smarter cities" mean nothing to people experiencing a hot day, a rainstorm, or tired legs. What if the city's infrastructure itself became IBM's advertising medium?

## Mechanic
Ogilvy France created OOH installations that were also functional urban infrastructure: a billboard that became a rain shelter with a bench, a poster that directed pedestrians through a ramp, a billboard that formed a seating ledge. Each incorporated the Smarter Planet logo. The design IS the proof.

## Why it worked
P17 (Design as Idea): the outdoor unit is not a picture of a smarter city — it IS a smarter city intervention, in miniature. The gap between "advertising that claims" and "advertising that does" is eliminated. IBM's competency is demonstrated in the format of the ad.

## Steal
When your brand's promise is about improving public infrastructure or human experience, make your advertising do exactly that — at whatever scale your budget allows. The smallest functional improvement outperforms the largest claim.

# IHOB (IHOP · 2018)

## Insight
IHOP is famous for pancakes but wants to sell burgers. No one would pay attention to "IHOP launches burgers." But if IHOP announced it was changing its name to IHOB (International House of Burgers), the internet would debate the decision for days before anyone revealed it was a stunt to promote the new burger menu.

## Mechanic
IHOP changed its name to IHOB across social media, signage, and public communications — letting the internet debate whether this was real. The reveal (it was a burger promotion) came after days of earned media, celebrity commentary, and competitor brands piling on. Burger sales increased 4x in the period following.

## Why it worked
Enemy or conflict (P02): the rename created an implicit conflict — loyalty to the pancake identity vs. the burger future — that the internet fought on IHOP's behalf for free. Absurd as carrier (P11): the stunt's premise was inherently ridiculous (a pancake house becoming a burger house), making it newsworthy and shareable. The ambiguity of whether it was real was the mechanism — once resolved, the reveal rewarded engagement.

## Steal
A brand stunt that people cannot definitively decode as real or fake will generate more earned media than one that is obviously either. The period of uncertainty is the campaign. Let the internet argue your brief.

# Beds (IKEA · 2018)

## Insight
The bed is the most intimate piece of furniture in any home — the site of sleep, sex, arguments, illness, and comfort. IKEA sells beds; the brand can approach that intimacy with either sentimentality or absurdist self-awareness.

## Mechanic
A short film using IKEA beds as the comic prop — exploring the various human situations that happen in, around, or because of a bed, with IKEA's characteristic tone of gentle absurdism.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) applied to domestic intimacy — IKEA is at home in the most literal space, and the absurdist approach to that space is more charming than the sentimental approach that competitor furniture brands take. The execution is lightweight but consistent with brand tone.

## Steal
The most intimate product categories are the ones most prone to sentimentality — which means absurdism is the bigger differentiator in those categories, not in categories that are already comic.

# Bookbook (IKEA · 2014)

## Insight
The physical IKEA catalogue was considered old-fashioned in a digital era — seen as a liability rather than an asset.

## Mechanic
A film parodying Apple product launch videos, presenting the IKEA print catalogue as a revolutionary new technology: "eternal battery life," "intuitive touch interface," "no lag," "tactile haptics." The joke is that all Apple's language applies literally to a paper catalogue.

## Why it worked
By treating the product's "weakness" (being non-digital) as its defining innovation, IKEA inverted the cultural hierarchy between print and digital. The Apple parody borrowed Apple's cultural authority and reflected it back with irony. The catalogue became shareable content rather than junk mail.

## Steal
Reframe an analogue or old-fashioned product feature using the aspirational language of a high-status adjacent category. The contrast between the grandiose language and the simple object is the joke — and the argument.
