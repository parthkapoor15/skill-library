# T-Shirts (IKEA · 2018)

## Insight
IKEA's product names — unpronounceable Swedish words to most of the world — are a well-known source of gentle frustration. Making that frustration the joke is more charming than pretending the products have universally legible names.

## Mechanic
A short film or social content playing with IKEA product names printed on T-shirts — using the absurdity of Swedish product nomenclature as a gentle self-deprecating comedy prop.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) via self-directed brand humour. IKEA's willingness to poke fun at its own naming convention (a well-known cultural joke about the brand) demonstrates confidence and self-awareness. The execution is lightweight but on-brand.

## Steal
Every brand has a well-known weakness that culture already jokes about. Own the joke before someone else does — self-directed comedy earns more goodwill than defensive communication.

# The Next Rembrandt (ING · 2016)

## Insight

ING finances creativity and innovation — but financial brands are invisible in culture. What if ING demonstrated the power of data by doing something no one believed possible: painting a new Rembrandt?

## Mechanic

18 months, 168,263 Rembrandt fragments analyzed by deep-learning AI. The result: a brand-new Rembrandt painting — same style, same period, same subject — 3D-printed and exhibited. ING positioned as the enabler of impossible creative acts.

## Why it worked

Tech as canvas meets design as idea: the project was simultaneously a genuine art-world event and an ad. The AI process was the story; the painting was the proof. It occupied news, galleries, and tech press simultaneously — sectors a financial brand never reaches.

## Steal

Use data/AI to create something that belongs in culture rather than media. The project must be genuinely worth discussing outside the advertising context. If it only works as an ad, it hasn't reached this level.

# The Beauty Inside (Intel · 2012)

## Insight
Intel's brand problem: consumers never see the chip, never think about it, and cannot easily articulate why Intel's chips matter over competitors'. The insight is that identity is what's inside — not what's outside. A character who wakes up every day in a different body, but is always the same person inside, is the most literal possible expression of "Intel Inside."

## Mechanic
A six-episode social film series about Alex, a person who wakes up each day in a different body — different age, gender, race, physical appearance — but maintains a continuous inner identity. The twist: any Facebook user can apply to star in the series as one of Alex's bodies. 107 people were cast via Facebook submissions. The films were shot and released over six weeks. Over 70 million total social reach. Won Cannes Grand Prix for branded content.

## Why it worked
P18 (tech as canvas): Facebook's casting infrastructure is the technology — without social media participation mechanics, this is a conventional film. With it, it becomes a social object. P16 (long-form drama): six episodes allow genuine character development; Alex's search for human connection despite a fractured physical identity is an emotionally complete story. The brand metaphor — identity is what's inside, not what's visible — maps precisely onto Intel's positioning without a single product shot.

## Steal
Find a human story whose emotional truth is a direct metaphor for your product's functional proposition. The beauty inside is not a tagline — it is the film's actual dramatic premise. When the metaphor and the story are the same thing, the brand message lands without advertising.

# Inglorious Fruits and Vegetables (Intermarché · 2014)

## Insight
30% of produce is discarded before reaching stores because it fails cosmetic standards. Consumers have been trained to reject misshapen food on appearance alone, not quality. Nobody challenged that conditioning because it was invisible — the ugly produce was never shown.

## Mechanic
Intermarché created a dedicated in-store section selling visually imperfect fruits and vegetables at 30% discount. Each variety had a campaign name ("The Ugly Carrot," "The Ridiculous Potato"), packaging with personality, and recipes demonstrating that ugly produces identical food to beautiful. Supported by TV, print, and tasting events.

## Why it worked
The inversion target was maximally precise: the cosmetic standard, not the food industry broadly. By creating a dedicated display with its own branding, Intermarché made "ugly produce" desirable rather than charity. The 30% discount gave rational permission to the emotional reframing. Sales of the non-ugly produce also increased — shoppers reconsidered the whole category.

## Steal
When an industry convention causes a measurable social harm, make that harm visible and offer an alternative in the same breath. Don't campaign against the convention — design around it. Product-as-solution, not communications-as-criticism.

# Jack's Back (Jack in the Box · 1994)

## Insight

In 1993, a Jack in the Box E.coli outbreak killed four children and hospitalized hundreds — the worst food safety disaster in fast food history. Conventional crisis PR called for apology and quiet rehabilitation. Chiat/Day's counterintuitive insight: lean into the rebirth. Jack in the Box's mascot (the clown head) had been "blown up" in a previous ad — now Jack was literally coming back from the dead, with a new suit, new attitude, and new focus on quality. Crisis as rebirth, not as shame.

## Mechanic

A Super Bowl TV spot in January 1994: the Jack mascot character walks out of a boardroom meeting, plants a Jack in the Box antenna ball on a limousine's antenna, and drives away. Tagline: "Jack's Back." The character Jack Box was then developed as the company's primary spokesman — irreverent, self-aware, anti-corporate — for the next two decades. The campaign is credited with rebuilding the brand after catastrophe.

## Why it worked

P11 (absurd as carrier): the absurdist Jack character gave the brand a voice that was fundamentally unserious about everything except quality — a combination that made the post-crisis messaging credible precisely because it wasn't defensive. P16 (long-form drama): the character developed across years into a genuinely beloved figure. The rebirth narrative — Jack blown up, Jack returning — gave the campaign an epic structure that made subsequent advertising feel like chapters in a continuing story.

## Steal

When a brand must rebuild after crisis, the most dangerous option is conventional contrition — it confirms the damage by dwelling on it. Consider instead a complete character rebirth: acknowledge the past structurally, then pivot fully to the future. The audience wants permission to like you again; give them a new reason rather than asking for forgiveness.
