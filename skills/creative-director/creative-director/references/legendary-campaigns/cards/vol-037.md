# What the Truck? (Furphy · 2023)

## Insight
Australian pub culture runs on tall tales and exaggeration — Furphy beer is literally named after a type of dubious rumor. The brand had a built-in license to lean into the absurd and pull audiences into the game.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign built around an absurdist truck stunt and participatory elements, inviting audiences to engage with Furphy's mythology of outrageous yarns. Humor and community storytelling were the engine; the truck was the physical anchor.

## Why it worked
Furphy's brand DNA (a "furphy" = an Australian tall tale) means absurdism is not decoration but brand essence. P11 and P09 working together: the absurd premise gives people something to talk about; the participation mechanic gives them a role in the yarn.

## Steal
When the brand name is itself a cultural artifact, make that artifact the campaign mechanic. Don't explain the heritage — activate it.

# 23 vs. 39 (Gatorade · 2009)

## Insight
Michael Jordan at 23 was the greatest athlete alive. Michael Jordan at 39, past retirement, playing again — what would happen? The insight is that the legend's performance at peak is incomparable to any human rival, but what about the legend against himself across time? That is the only match-up worth watching.

## Mechanic
A film pitting a digitally recreated 23-year-old Michael Jordan against the 39-year-old Michael Jordan in a one-on-one game. Both versions animated and stylized. The competition is between the greatest player alive and the greatest version of himself — implying that Gatorade fuels athletes across decades, not just in their prime.

## Why it worked
P16 (long-form drama): the emotional weight comes from the mythological confrontation — prime Jordan vs. older Jordan. P05 (cultural hijack): Jordan returning from retirement was a cultural event; the campaign inserts itself into that narrative by asking the question the entire sports world was already asking. The film extends the brand's long association with Jordan without relying on product placement.

## Steal
When your brand has a long-standing relationship with a cultural legend, create a confrontation between versions of that legend. It is more interesting than a simple tribute and more memorable than a celebrity endorsement.

# Be Like Mike (Gatorade · 1991)

## Insight

Every child who has played basketball has, at some point, imagined being Michael Jordan. Not as a delusion but as a pure aspiration — the complete absorption into what it would feel like to move that way, to make those decisions, to exist at that level of mastery. Gatorade's insight was that this aspiration is not embarrassing — it is the foundational feeling of sport itself.

## Mechanic

A TV campaign built around the jingle "Be Like Mike" — montages of Jordan playing basketball intercut with children, adults, and celebrities imitating his moves in ordinary settings. The jingle became immediately iconic, and the phrase "Be Like Mike" entered common usage as a shorthand for aspirational excellence. The campaign ran in various forms for years and is one of the most recognized sports advertising campaigns in US history.

## Why it worked

P13 (truth telling): stating plainly that people want to be like Jordan was audacious — most brands would hedge or soften it. Gatorade said it directly and made it the campaign's organizing principle. P16 (long-form drama) used the jingle as an emotional anchor that made each subsequent execution feel like part of a continuing experience. The campaign transformed a functional sports drink into a symbol of the aspiration to excellence.

## Steal

Name what the audience already wants without making them feel foolish for wanting it. The aspiration your audience has toward your product's hero or territory — if stated plainly — is more powerful than any product claim, because the audience already believes it.

# Replay (Gatorade · 2009)

## Insight
Middle-aged men carry one sporting moment their whole lives — the high school game they drew, the championship that got away. Gatorade is the fuel of performance, but its audience is largely people whose peak performance is behind them. What if Gatorade could give them one more chance?

## Mechanic
Gatorade identified a real high school football rivalry — a 1993 game that ended in a tie — found the original players (now in their mid-30s, some out of shape), trained them for months, and replayed the game. The documentary captured the whole journey: physical decline, family sacrifice, sports medicine, and the emotional weight of unfinished business. The game was played again. For real.

## Why it worked
P13 (truth-telling) operated at its most powerful: the truth that athletic prime ends but competitive desire doesn't. P16 (long-form) was structurally required — the months of training were the emotional story, not just context for the game. The campaign was a non-advertising idea (a real event) that functioned as Gatorade's most honest product demonstration: the drink is for people who still want to compete, regardless of age. The human stakes made it genuinely moving rather than promotional.

## Steal
When your brand's core promise is performance, find a performance that matters to real people — not manufactured athletes — and make it happen. The authenticity of real stakes produces emotion that no scripted film can match.

# Bell (GE · 2016)

## Insight
GE's brand of industrial innovation is disruptive — once it changes an industry, there is no going back. "You can't unring a bell" captures that irreversibility of technological progress.

## Mechanic
A film exploring the premise of an unringable bell through absurdist, extended narrative logic — likely combining literal bell imagery with surreal consequences and a narrative that escalates the absurdity, ending on a product or brand truth.

## Why it worked
Absurdism (P11) combined with long-form structure (P16) allows GE to communicate the spirit of irreversible technological change through a surreal story rather than a product demonstration. The approach makes industrial innovation feel playful and culturally relevant.

## Steal
Use idioms and clichés about change ("can't unring a bell," "cat out of the bag") as a literal absurdist premise for a film. The audience's familiarity with the phrase makes the visual absurdism land immediately.
