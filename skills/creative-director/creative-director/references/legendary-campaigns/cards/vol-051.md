# Cook This Page (IKEA · 2017)

## Insight
IKEA sells kitchen products. Recipe books and food print are entirely separate categories. But what if the recipe could be cooked on the page itself — turning an ad into a functional cooking tool?

## Mechanic
IKEA created parchment-paper recipe pages printed with food items at scale. Readers could place the page directly in a baking dish, add the ingredients shown, and cook it. The print ad becomes the recipe card and the baking liner simultaneously.

## Why it worked
Design as idea (P17): the ad's material and format IS the product utility demonstration. Behaviour inversion (P03): the ad is consumed by cooking rather than read. The execution is elegant, cheap to produce, and generates enormous share value through its unexpected functionality.

## Steal
Ask what your ad could DO beyond communicate. When the physical form of the communication can be repurposed as a tool that demonstrates the product's use case, you've found a design idea, not a communication idea.

# Ghosts (IKEA · 2018)

## Insight
IKEA furniture is so ubiquitous it inhabits spaces its owners barely notice. The absurdist premise: even ghosts, given the chance to haunt somewhere, would choose IKEA furniture for its familiarity and comfort.

## Mechanic
A short film treating IKEA's home environment as the default setting even for supernatural beings — ghosts choosing IKEA products for comfort. The domesticity of IKEA and the otherworldliness of ghosts create the comedy.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) with a soft supernatural premise. IKEA's established visual world is strong enough to absorb the incongruity of ghosts without losing brand coherence. The premise is a gentle joke about how thoroughly IKEA has colonised the concept of "home."

## Steal
When your brand's products are so associated with a concept (home, comfort, domesticity) that even impossible scenarios default to them, that cultural saturation is the premise for absurdist comedy.

# Irresistible Pointless Ads (IKEA · 2018)

## Insight
The most watched YouTube videos are often the most boring: hour-long rain sounds, fireplace loops, ASMR unboxings. IKEA's products — shelves, sofas, mattresses — are objects of quiet domestic satisfaction, not excitement.

## Mechanic
IKEA produced intentionally long, slow, "boring" product films — 25-minute tours of a bookshelf, a sofa demonstration with no music, a mattress video with gentle narration. Framed as ASMR content, they were seductive for exactly the audience category advertising ignores: people who just want to chill.

## Why it worked
Behaviour inversion (making deliberately dull ads) and limitation as power (the brand's most "boring" products become content worth watching) work in combination. The meta-awareness that these are ads-that-aren't-ads makes them inherently shareable. They reached insomnia audiences, ASMR enthusiasts, and people who avoid ads — all at once.

## Steal
Ask what your audience is actually watching at 11pm. If it's not your category's content, find a way to make your product appear in that native context rather than interrupting it.

# Lamp (IKEA · 2002)

## Insight
People form sentimental attachments to old objects — lamps, chairs, cups — long past their useful life. This sentimentality is irrational and stops people from upgrading to better things. IKEA could use that irrationality against itself.

## Mechanic
A 60-second film directed by Spike Jonze: an old red lamp is carried to the curb and replaced. The camera lingers on the lamp in the rain as a sad orchestral score plays. A Swedish man breaks into frame: "Many of you feel bad for this lamp. That is because you are crazy. It has no feelings. And the new one is much better." The joke lands because the film earned the emotion it then punctured.

## Why it worked
The film spent 55 seconds sincerely building pathos for a lamp — using every emotional cue of a drama — then destroyed the sentimental frame in five seconds. The humor worked because it was earned, not cheap. More importantly, it addressed the actual behavioral barrier to purchasing IKEA furniture: guilt about discarding the old. By calling that guilt "crazy," it gave consumers permission to upgrade without shame. The absurdist twist (P11) was the delivery mechanism for a genuinely strategic message (P16 structure, emotional build).

## Steal
Identify the emotional barrier preventing a rational action. Dramatize that barrier so sincerely the audience recognizes themselves. Then puncture it with precision. The self-awareness of the puncture is what earns the laugh — and removes the barrier.

# Playin' With My Friends (IKEA · 2013)

## Insight
IKEA products furnish the spaces where children play and adults socialise — but no one thinks of furniture as the enabler of connection. The insight: the best childhood memories happen in the most ordinary domestic spaces, made extraordinary by who's in them.

## Mechanic
A film showing children playing with IKEA furniture in ways that transform it into imaginative worlds — forts, stages, ships — while a version of "Playin' With My Friends" plays on the soundtrack. The furniture is not the hero; the play it enables is.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) gently — the children's imaginative transformations of ordinary furniture into extraordinary play spaces is the comedy and the truth simultaneously. P16 gives the film enough time to build genuine warmth. IKEA's functional products disappear into the background, which is exactly the right brand move: the furniture is so good at being invisible that the life around it becomes vivid.

## Steal
When your product enables a human behaviour, let the behaviour be the hero and the product be the stage. The most invisible brand communication is often the most effective.
