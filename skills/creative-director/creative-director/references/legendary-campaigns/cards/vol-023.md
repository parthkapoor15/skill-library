# Surgeon (Clash of Clans · 2015)

## Insight
Serious professionals who play mobile games during off-hours represent an under-acknowledged audience. The contrast between a high-stakes real-world identity and a raiding fantasy game creates productive absurdity.

## Mechanic
A spot in the Clash of Clans campaign series featuring a surgeon character in a surgical setting, treating the mobile game with the same gravity as a real-life high-stakes scenario. Part of the wider "even people like you play" execution family.

## Why it worked
Part of the "Revenge" campaign ecosystem, this execution extended the core insight — anyone can be drawn into Clash — into professional archetypes. The juxtaposition of the surgeon's gravitas and the game's stakes generated quick recognition and shareability.

## Steal
When the audience is broader than the stereotype, use archetype contrast to expand perceived relevance without changing the product. Show the unexpected player, not the expected one.

# Border (Coca-Cola · 2012)

## Insight
Political divisions and border conflicts dominate news cycles, but people on either side of most borders have more in common than the conflict suggests. Shared moments of ordinary pleasure — including a cold drink — exist on both sides of disputed lines.

## Mechanic
A film set at a geographical or political border, showing people from different sides sharing or connecting over a Coca-Cola. The drink functions as a universal social lubricant that transcends the political division.

## Why it worked
Coca-Cola has built its brand around human connection for decades — the "Hilltop" tradition. The border setting makes the connection claim maximally legible by placing it against the strongest available symbol of division. The political charge is real enough to give the act of sharing weight.

## Steal
When your brand stands for connection, stage the connection at the point of maximum disconnection. The contrast between the division (the setting) and the act (sharing) carries the argument without requiring any copy.

# Christmas Caravan (Coca-Cola · 1995)

## Insight

Coca-Cola had been associating itself with Christmas since the 1930s — it was Haddon Sundblom's Coca-Cola Santa illustrations that helped solidify the modern image of Santa Claus in red. By 1995, the brand could claim Christmas as genuine cultural territory rather than borrowed relevance. The Caravan is not an ad for Coke; it is the annual arrival of something people actually wait for.

## Mechanic

A TV film and subsequent real-world truck tour: illuminated red Coca-Cola trucks driving through snowy landscapes at night, with the tagline "Holidays Are Coming." The music became inseparable from the imagery. The trucks became a literal physical experience — the "Coca-Cola Christmas Truck Tour" visiting UK cities annually. Audiences queued hours to see the trucks.

## Why it worked

P10 (serialization and ritual): the Caravan created a genuine calendar ritual — audiences began treating the TV airing as the unofficial start of Christmas. P16 (long-form drama): the film's slow, cinematic movement built anticipation through atmosphere rather than message. The campaign proved that a brand can own a cultural moment so completely that the moment is incomplete without the brand's presence.

## Steal

If a brand has years of consistent seasonal presence, the goal shifts from "getting attention" to "activating an established ritual." The audience's anticipation does more work than any creative element. Build for the ritual first; content is secondary.

# First Taste (Coca-Cola · 2017)

## Insight
The first taste of Coca-Cola is unforgettable — but it is also unrepeatable. Every subsequent taste measures itself against that first experience. The emotional power of the brand lives in the memory of the first encounter.

## Mechanic
A film exploring the emotional experience of encountering Coca-Cola for the first time — told across different people in different situations, with the shared recognition of that specific sensory memory as the connecting thread.

## Why it worked
P16 (long-form drama) building emotional territory around the product's most powerful brand truth: the sensory memory of first experience. P09 (user as co-author) through the shared recognition mechanic — audiences recall their own first taste and project it onto the film. The campaign activates personal memory without needing to manufacture a new one.

## Steal
Every product has a "first encounter" that its most loyal users remember. Making that memory the content of a campaign activates a personal archive that no other brand can access. You are not creating an emotion; you are unlocking one that already exists.
