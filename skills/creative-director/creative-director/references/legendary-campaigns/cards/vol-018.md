# Is Cadbury's Drumming Gorilla the Best Ad of All Time? (Cadbury · 2015)

## Insight
This entry refers to the 2015 Guardian article and likely a re-run or anniversary replay of the original 2007 Gorilla spot. The cultural reassessment of the Gorilla campaign — years after its original airing — demonstrates the longevity of the P11+P16+P07 mechanic.

## Mechanic
Anniversary or cultural reassessment of the original "Gorilla" spot (2007). The Guardian article re-examined the campaign's impact eight years later, often coinciding with re-airings or retrospective media coverage. The card documents the meta-cultural life of the original spot.

## Why it worked
Great absurdist work with genuine emotional resonance ages better than trend-dependent work. The Gorilla remained legible and pleasurable eight years later because its core mechanism — build tension, release through joy — is structural, not referential. It didn't depend on a meme, a news peg, or a cultural moment that would expire.

## Steal
When evaluating long-term creative value: ask whether the spot depends on contextual relevance (a moment, a trend) or structural mechanism (tension-release, emotional arc). Structural work ages; contextual work expires.

# Made to Share (Cadbury · 2024)

## Insight
Cadbury's Dairy Milk bar has a segmented design that is universally recognisable — but the segments are never equal, and sharing chocolate always involves a negotiation. The insight: the act of deciding who gets which piece is itself an expression of relationships.

## Mechanic
An integrated campaign built around the physical design of the Cadbury bar — the segments — as a sharing mechanic. The campaign included customisable packaging, OOH that visualised the sharing decision, and social content that made the chocolate-sharing negotiation the content itself.

## Why it worked
P17 (design as idea): the product's existing physical design becomes the campaign's structural mechanic — the segments were always there, the campaign made them mean something specific. P09 (user as co-author): the personalisation mechanic and social content generation turned a purchase into a shareable moment. The campaign found creative territory that no other confectionery brand had access to because the bar's design is proprietary.

## Steal
Your product's physical design contains ideas that competitors cannot copy. Map every design element — shape, structure, portion — and ask what social or emotional behaviour each one enables that has never been amplified.

# Got Milk? (California Milk Processor Board · 1993)

## Insight
Nobody thinks about milk until they don't have it. The insight is deprivation, not benefit — milk's value is only understood in its absence. Every positive claim about milk was already known and ignored; the only fresh angle was the moment of desperate need when the carton is empty.

## Mechanic
A campaign built around deprivation scenarios — the most famous a TV spot showing an obsessive Aaron Burr historian eating a peanut butter sandwich when the radio calls for the answer to a trivia question worth $10,000: "Who shot Alexander Hamilton?" He knows the answer but cannot speak through the peanut butter — he has no milk. Tagline: "Got milk?" The two-word question became one of the most parodied and licensed taglines in advertising history. Extended into a decade of print ads with celebrities wearing milk mustaches.

## Why it worked
P09 (user as co-author): the tagline became a cultural template — "Got [X]?" entered the vernacular and was adopted by thousands of unrelated contexts, extending the campaign's reach infinitely beyond its paid media. P10 (serialization): the milk mustache print campaign ran for 20+ years with rotating celebrities, creating annual media events around each new execution. The deprivation insight was inexhaustible — any situation where milk would be needed could produce a new execution.

## Steal
If the positive benefits of your product are already known and dismissed, try communicating through the negative — the moment of absence, the experience of not having it. Loss aversion is stronger than gain motivation. "Got Milk?" does not tell you why milk is good; it shows you why its absence is catastrophic.

# The Last Photo (CALM · 2022)

## Insight
The cultural image of someone at risk of suicide is a visibly distressed, withdrawn individual — but the last photos of most people who die by suicide show them smiling, laughing, apparently happy.

## Mechanic
An exhibition and campaign presenting real final photographs of people who died by suicide — joyful, ordinary images — alongside text from their families. The contrast between what was visible and what was hidden destroys the mental model that keeps people from seeking help or offering it.

## Why it worked
The campaign attacks a specific cognitive bias (I would recognise if someone was at risk) with direct, irrefutable visual evidence. It is not an argument — it is a fact presented without decoration. Each smiling face is a counter-proof.

## Steal
Attack a specific, named cognitive bias with the minimum evidence required to disprove it. When the bias is destroyed, the desired behaviour becomes obvious without being prescribed.

# The March of the Emperors (Canal+ · 2006)

## Insight
The nature documentary format carries a peculiar cultural authority — it makes anything filmed in its style feel important and worth watching. Canal+ subscribers, as cinema lovers, would appreciate a brand that uses the format's authority as a joke rather than a crutch.

## Mechanic
A spot parodying the style of "March of the Penguins" (the 2005 documentary hit). Penguins march in the familiar fashion, narrated with documentary gravitas — until they arrive at a cinema and queue for tickets. The joke is earned because the format is respected, not mocked. Canal+'s tagline: "Committed to Cinema."

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): the cultural context of the original film makes the parody work — audiences who saw "March of the Penguins" experience the delight of recognition. P16 (long-form drama): the documentary format has its own emotional logic, which the spot borrows and then playfully undermines. BETC's Canal+ work established the template that "The Bear" would perfect five years later.

## Steal
Parody works only when you respect the original. The penguins going to the cinema is funny because the preceding shots are genuinely good nature-documentary filmmaking. A cheap parody would have been a cheaper joke.
