# #OptOutside (REI · 2015)

## Insight
Black Friday is the commercial peak of the retail calendar — every outdoor retailer competes on deals. But REI's brand exists to get people outside, which is the opposite of what Black Friday demands.

## Mechanic
REI closed all stores and its website on Black Friday 2015, paid employees for the day, and called on the public to opt outside instead of shopping. The campaign hashtag became a mass movement; partner brands and public lands joined.

## Why it worked
The brand acted on its stated values at direct commercial cost — giving up one of the biggest retail days of the year. The sacrifice is the proof. No amount of messaging about the outdoors equals closing 143 stores on Black Friday.

## Steal
Identify the single day when your brand's values most directly contradict the category's commercial convention. Act against the convention, publicly, at visible cost. The action is the campaign.

# The Soy Sauce Posters (Sagawa Shoyu · 2019)

## Insight
Soy sauce stains are among the most stubborn stains known to Japanese cooking culture. The stain itself — the mark that soy sauce leaves — is the most honest proof of the product's character.

## Mechanic
Sagawa Shoyu created posters using actual soy sauce as ink — the brand's product IS the design medium. The stains, rings, and drips formed the visual identity. The poster communicates quality through the physical evidence of the product's own character.

## Why it worked
The medium is the message at its most literal: soy sauce advertising made from soy sauce. The material choice is self-referential without irony. The aged, natural appearance of the stain-designed poster communicates craft and tradition more effectively than any claim.

## Steal
When your product leaves a physical mark or trace, use that mark as your design element. The product's own evidence of use is the most honest creative material available.

# Christmas is For Sharing (Sainsbury's · 2014)

## Insight
The 1914 Christmas Truce — when soldiers on both sides laid down arms, crossed no-man's land, and played football — is the most powerful evidence that humanity refuses to be destroyed by war. Sharing a bar of chocolate across enemy lines is the simplest and most devastating illustration of what Christmas actually means.

## Mechanic
A feature-quality period war film recreating the 1914 Christmas Truce, made in collaboration with the Royal British Legion. A German soldier and a British soldier share a chocolate bar in no-man's land. Real historical events, real characters (loosely), cinematographic quality. The Sainsbury's chocolate bar — a replica of a real 1914 wartime edition — was sold in stores, with all profits going to the Royal British Legion.

## Why it worked
Every decision was load-bearing: the historical moment (authentic), the collaboration (credible), the product connection (not forced — chocolate was in rations), the charity tie-in (meaning real cost to the brand), the production quality (respect, not nostalgia-wash). The result was the most-viewed Christmas ad in history at that point.

## Steal
If you're going to use a historical event in advertising, the minimum requirement is authentic collaboration with the institutions that own the memory. "Inspired by" is not enough. Sainsbury's worked with the Royal British Legion. That collaboration was the moral basis for the film.

# Beer Tooth (Salta · 2019)

## Insight
Opening a beer bottle with your teeth is a universal bar ritual — a casual display of confidence and camaraderie. The bottle cap is the prop; your teeth are the tool.

## Mechanic
Salta created OOH posters designed to actually open beer bottles — the poster surface had a bottle-cap opener integrated into its design at exactly the right height. The ad becomes the utility.

## Why it worked
Design as idea: the poster is a beer opener. The integration of function into format is seamless and relevant — a beer brand that helps you open beers when you have one in hand. The execution is executed simply and cheaply.

## Steal
Find one practical moment in your audience's relationship with your product category. Design your communication to solve that moment rather than describe it.

# Busy Busy Busy (Samsung · 2016)

## Insight
The modern professional's self-declared busyness is a performance of status, not a description of productivity. Samsung phones are tools for that performance as much as for actual work.

## Mechanic
A comedic film depicting an absurdist spiral of "busy" — a character whose busyness escalates to absurd extremes, each device feature enabling further performative occupation. The humour targets the self-aware viewer who recognises themselves in the satire.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier) let Samsung poke at the exact behaviour its product enables — a rare instance of a tech brand owning the cultural critique of tech use with enough self-awareness to be charming rather than defensive. The self-referential quality made the ad shareable.

## Steal
If your product enables a behaviour that culture simultaneously celebrates and mocks, address both sides at once. The self-aware brand that says "we know what you do with our product" earns more trust than the brand that only shows idealized use.
