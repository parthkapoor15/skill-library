# Michael CeraVe (CeraVe · 2024)

## Insight
CeraVe is a dermatologist-developed skincare brand. Its name sounds like Michael Cera. What if Michael Cera was behind CeraVe all along, and had simply been waiting for the right moment to claim credit?

## Mechanic
In the weeks before the Super Bowl, content appeared online suggesting Michael Cera had developed CeraVe. He was photographed at dermatology conferences, endorsed the products in awkward videos, and claimed ownership. Then the Super Bowl ad "revealed" the truth. The campaign was a coordinated PR fiction.

## Why it worked
The campaign exploited the exact gap between the brand name and the actor's name — a coincidence so obvious no one had acted on it. The cultural hijack (P05) hijacks the actor's persona; the conflict (P02) is the fake controversy between CeraVe's actual origin and Cera's claim. The joke is structurally perfect: the reveal satisfies because the setup built genuine uncertainty.

## Steal
Name-based coincidences are usually ignored because they feel too simple. They're not. If you have a coincidence, commit to it entirely before the reveal. The audience should genuinely not know whether it's real.

# Share the Fantasy (Chanel No. 5 · 1979)

## Insight

Luxury fragrance is not about smell — it is about the self you believe you could inhabit. Chanel No. 5's insight was that its aspiration was not realistic or grounded; it was explicitly a fantasy — and rather than hiding this, the brand should celebrate and share it.

## Mechanic

A 30-second TV film directed by Ridley Scott: a woman in a white dress by a pool, a man diving in, surreal editing, a dreamlike visual language with no narrative resolution. The film is closer to an art object than a commercial — associative, non-linear, unresolved. No product claims. The bottle appears at the end. Scott's direction was so cinematic it read as short film rather than advertising.

## Why it worked

P16 (long-form drama): by borrowing the visual language of European art cinema, the film transferred that cultural capital to the brand. Chanel wasn't making an ad — it was making a visual tone poem that happened to end with the product. The unresolved narrative was a feature, not a bug: the audience was invited to complete the fantasy themselves, making the brand's territory infinitely personal.

## Steal

For luxury brands, aspiration is not served by explanation. The more precisely you describe the fantasy, the less powerful it becomes. Withhold resolution. Let the audience project themselves into the incomplete image — their version of the fantasy is always more compelling than yours.

# The Lost Class (ChangeTheRef · 2022)

## Insight
3,044 students would have graduated from Parkland, Stoneman Douglas, and other schools affected by mass shootings in 2021, but never did. Their absence is abstract — until made concrete.

## Mechanic
ChangeTheRef (founded by Parkland parents) hired actors to pose as gun rights advocates and invited two prominent NRA board members to give a commencement address — to 3,044 empty chairs representing students killed by gun violence. The board members didn't know who they were actually speaking to. The speeches were filmed and released.

## Why it worked
The social experiment exposes the gap between rhetoric and reality in real time. The board members' discomfort when confronted with the specific human cost of their positions is the evidence. The empty chairs make the abstraction physical. No CGI, no actors playing victims — just presence through absence.

## Steal
When your cause is dismissed as abstract, find the single visual that makes the numbers physically undeniable. Then construct an experience where your opponent must face that visual while doing something they'd normally do comfortably.

# Idents (Channel 4 · 2024)

## Insight
A broadcaster's channel ident is normally a visual formality — an overlooked transition element that signals nothing beyond ownership. If the "4" logo was made to mean something every time it appeared, it could function as a continuous brand manifesto.

## Mechanic
A series of idents where the "4" numeral assembles itself from images representing Channel 4's values: disability representation, queer culture, bizarre humour, alternative perspectives. Each ident is a distinct micro-story, 15–30 seconds, broadcast between programmes.

## Why it worked
The brand's "alternative" identity is demonstrated every few minutes of broadcast rather than stated once per campaign. Repetition of diverse visual content becomes the brand argument. Routine becomes ritual; formality becomes philosophy.

## Steal
Convert a repetitive brand touchpoint (ident, receipt footer, loading screen, packaging seal) into a platform for micro-stories. When something appears hundreds of times, even a small idea compounds.

# Meet the Superhumans (Channel 4 · 2012)

## Insight
The Paralympic Games were seen as the lesser, inspirational sibling of the Olympics — a charity-toned event for people who admire disabled athletes from a comfortable distance of pity.

## Mechanic
A film using the visual and sonic codes of high-intensity sports advertising — aggressive editing, Public Enemy soundtrack, close-up action footage — applied entirely to Paralympic athletes. No soft tones, no inspiration porn, no charity framing.

## Why it worked
The borrowed codes of a higher-status category (mainstream sports advertising) instantly reframed the Paralympic athlete from object of sympathy to subject of power. Category hijack via visual language.

## Steal
Apply the code of a higher-status adjacent category to an undervalued subject. The audience reads the visual language first and the subject second — by the time they process who they're watching, the respect is already installed.
