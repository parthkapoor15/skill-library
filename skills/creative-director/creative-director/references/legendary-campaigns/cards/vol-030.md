# Spicy But Not Too Spicy (Doritos · 2024)

## Insight
The category of spicy food marketing has become dominated by extreme heat-focused machismo — every brand competes on intensity. The majority of consumers actually want flavour with accessible heat.

## Mechanic
A parody ad featuring Walton Goggins that mocks the aggressive "extreme spicy" advertising codes of the category, positioning Doritos as the self-aware alternative that delivers satisfying flavour without the macho performance of extreme heat culture.

## Why it worked
The category code inversion — using humour to mock the conventions every competitor was earnestly employing — positioned Doritos as culturally intelligent while competitors signalled insecurity through hyperbole.

## Steal
When a category has an over-earnest convention (extreme, intense, aggressive), be the first to parody it from within. Self-awareness about the category's absurdity reads as confidence.

# Most Interesting Man in the World (Dos Equis · 2006)

## Insight
Beer advertising for men defaults to either lowest-common-denominator humor or aspirational sports. Neither addresses the actual male fantasy: being genuinely interesting — worldly, experienced, capable of anything. The insight is that men do not want to be athletes; they want to be legends.

## Mechanic
A campaign built around a single character — a silver-haired, magnificently bearded man of indeterminate age — whose exploits are described in mock-epic narration. "He once parallel-parked a train." "Sharks have a week dedicated to him." Spots end with him raising a glass and saying "I don't always drink beer. But when I do, I prefer Dos Equis. Stay thirsty, my friends." The character is played completely straight, with zero winking at the audience.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): the hyperbolic exploits are so absurd they function as a parody of the masculine ideal while simultaneously celebrating it. P10 (serialization): the character built recognition and anticipation over years — each spot added to the legend without requiring knowledge of prior spots. The campaign turned a Mexican import with little brand equity into a cultural touchstone. Effiefward-winning sales growth: imports grew when all other Mexican beer brands declined.

## Steal
Build a character so internally consistent that the absurdity reads as logic. The Most Interesting Man works because he is never in on the joke — his dignity is the joke, and also the aspiration. Never break character. The campaign's failure would have been a knowing wink.

# Evolution (Dove · 2006)

## Insight
Every image of beauty in advertising is a lie — not because of retouching alone, but because the entire process, from casting to lighting to digital manipulation, is designed to produce an image that no real human could achieve. Women compare themselves to these images without knowing the images are impossible. The insight: show them the process.

## Mechanic
A 75-second time-lapse film showing the complete production of a billboard image: a plain woman sits down, professional makeup is applied, hair is styled, lighting is set, photos are taken, and then a digital artist stretches the neck, enlarges the eyes, slims the jaw. In 75 seconds, a real face becomes the impossible billboard face. Final card: "No wonder our perception of beauty is distorted." Posted to YouTube with no media spend. 2.4 million views in the first week. Eventually 16 million views — unprecedented for branded content in 2006.

## Why it worked
P12 (social experiment): the film is structured as a revelation — showing the backstage of an industry that depends on invisibility. P03 (behavior inversion): beauty advertising normally shows the beautiful result; this shows the constructed process. The brand's courage — a beauty brand exposing the beauty industry's deception — was its own news story. Won two Cannes Grand Prix.

## Steal
Find the process that your industry keeps hidden because its revelation would be uncomfortable. Show it. The transparency itself is the brand idea. The discomfort of the audience is not a risk to manage — it is the evidence that the insight is real.

# Little Girls (Dove · 2007)

## Insight
Girls as young as five internalize negative messages about their bodies from the images and narratives they see around them. They are not born with body image issues — they acquire them from culture. A brand that claims to fight for real beauty should acknowledge this acquisition at its earliest stage.

## Mechanic
A film showing young girls — barely old enough to read — describing their bodies in the language of adult female self-criticism: "My tummy is too big." "I hate my hair." "I wish I were prettier." The camera holds on their faces — innocent, utterly genuine. The effect is profoundly uncomfortable. The film closes with the proposition that low self-esteem in young women begins here, at this age, and that we can change it.

## Why it worked
P13 (truth telling): the truth being told — that body image anxiety starts in childhood — was not being stated anywhere in the beauty category. Dove had the courage to name it directly. P03 (behavior inversion): beauty advertising normally aspires; this confronts. The discomfort is not accidental — it is the mechanism by which the message is delivered and retained.

## Steal
When your brand's purpose intersects with a truth about children's experience, the stakes rise automatically. Audiences cannot dismiss discomfort about children the same way they dismiss discomfort about adults. The emotional weight is inherently amplified.

# Real Beauty Sketches (Dove · 2013)

## Insight
Women are their own harshest critics. When describing themselves to a stranger, they emphasize their perceived flaws — "my jaw is too wide," "I have a big forehead" — while others describe the same women with warmth and accuracy. The gap between self-perception and how others see you is a measurable, demonstrable truth about how women relate to their own appearance.

## Mechanic
An FBI-trained forensic artist sits behind a curtain. Women describe themselves; he draws from description only. Then a stranger who met the woman briefly describes the same woman; he draws again. The two portraits are placed side by side. The self-described portrait is always harsher, tighter, less open. The stranger's portrait is closer to beauty. Women see the difference and cry. 114 million YouTube views in the first week — the most watched branded video in history at the time.

## Why it worked
P12 (social experiment): the forensic artist creates genuine third-party credibility — this is not Dove telling women they are more beautiful than they think; this is an FBI-trained professional and a stranger demonstrating it. P03 (behavior inversion): beauty advertising shows external beauty; this reveals internal distortion. The emotional moment is earned through a structured experiment, not scripted drama. Cannes Grand Prix. Effie Grand Prix.

## Steal
Design the experiment so that the result could plausibly go the other way. Real Beauty Sketches works because the women might have described themselves accurately, or even generously — the fact that they do not is what makes the finding meaningful rather than manufactured. Credibility requires genuine risk of a different outcome.
