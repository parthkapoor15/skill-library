# Climate Name Change (350Action · 2014)

## Insight
The World Meteorological Organization names tropical storms after people. Climate change makes extreme weather more frequent and severe. Politicians who deny climate change while receiving fossil fuel money are making those storms worse — so they should bear the names.

## Mechanic
A petition campaign to the World Meteorological Organization to name extreme weather events after climate-denying politicians. For every storm, a politician's name was assigned based on their denial record and industry funding. Supporters shared "storm warnings" on social media.

## Why it worked
The mechanic literalized the consequence in an unmistakable and shareable way. Naming a hurricane after a senator made the abstraction of climate policy into a visceral, personal statement. The petition format gave people a low-friction action with high symbolic payoff.

## Steal
When a complex policy issue needs a human face, find the mechanism that links action to consequence directly. The more literal the link, the more memorable the statement. Attribution as activism.

# Absolut _____ (Absolut Vodka · 1981)

## Insight

Absolut arrived in the US market in 1979 with a bottle shape so distinctive it was nearly impossible to market — apothecary-style, short, squat, and unusual compared to every other spirit bottle. TBWA's insight: don't hide the bottle's weirdness. Make the bottle the idea itself, infinitely adaptable to any cultural context.

## Mechanic

A print campaign launched in 1981 that ran for 25 years: each execution showed the Absolut bottle (or its recognizable shape) reimagined for a city, a season, an artist, or a cultural moment. "Absolut L.A." — a bottle-shaped swimming pool. "Absolut Warhol" — commissioned pop art. "Absolut Chicago" — a wind-swept bottle. "Absolut Citron" — a lemon. Over 1,500 executions. The campaign ran without interruption for 25 years and is credited with making Absolut the #1 imported vodka in the US.

## Why it worked

P17 (design as idea): the bottle silhouette is so distinctive that it can be presented empty, inverted, distorted, or implied, and remain instantly recognizable. The design is the message. P07 (invisible brand): the actual bottle appears rarely in full — instead the brand's shape becomes a lens through which any cultural moment is reinterpreted. The campaign proved that a design language, consistently applied, is more durable than any creative concept.

## Steal

If your product has a distinctive physical form, that form is your infinite campaign: apply it to every cultural context your audience moves through. The audience does the creative work of completion — you only need to provide the silhouette and the setup.

# Love Has No Labels (Ad Council · 2015)

## Insight
Bias operates on appearance — remove the visual cues and what's left is the universal shape of human connection.

## Mechanic
A large X-ray screen erected in a public space shows only skeletons; behind it, diverse couples and families — LGBTQ+, interracial, interfaith, multigenerational — embrace and dance. Then each pair steps out from behind the screen, revealing themselves to the crowd.

## Why it worked
Viewers first experience love as undifferentiated human contact, then discover they have been watching something they might have prejudged. The audience catches their own bias in real time — it is a self-experiment, not a lecture.

## Steal
Hide identity, show shared humanity, then reveal identity. The sequence makes the audience discover the prejudice themselves rather than being told they have it.

# Like My Addiction (AddictAide · 2017)

## Insight
Alcoholics and addicts present their lives on social media with the same positive spin as everyone else — but the photos that look most enviable often contain the most damning evidence of a problem hiding in plain sight.

## Mechanic
A fake Instagram account run for months as a lifestyle influencer persona — attractive woman, parties, glamorous moments — all secretly documenting the progression of alcohol addiction. Followers liked, commented, and admired. When revealed, all content stayed up with captions rewritten to expose what had actually been shown.

## Why it worked
Cultural hijack (P05): the campaign lived inside the exact platform and aesthetic language the target audience already inhabited. Truth-telling (P13): the reveal made visible what the audience had been unconsciously endorsing. User as co-author (P09): the audience's own likes and comments became evidence of the problem.

## Steal
When the behaviour you want to change is visible on social media, the platform itself can be turned into both the evidence and the intervention. The audience's prior engagement becomes the twist — they were already participants before they knew it.

# Billie Jean King Your Shoes (Adidas · 2019)

## Insight

Gender pay gaps in sports are well-documented — but they rarely feel personal or immediate. What if the gap was made visible in the most ordinary workplace interaction?

## Mechanic

Campaign inviting people to confront workplace gender pay gaps by "Billie Jean King-ing" their shoes — a mechanic where women could send their work shoes to Adidas and have them replaced with equal-value shoes representing what they'd earn if paid the same as male colleagues.

## Why it worked

Absurdist participation meets user-as-co-author: by making the shoe a physical proxy for pay equity, the campaign turned an abstract economic issue into a tactile, personal action. Billie Jean King as symbol was earned, not borrowed.

## Steal

Find the physical object in your category that can stand in for a social inequality. Make the object the proof. Exchange mechanics work because they require real commitment from the participant.
