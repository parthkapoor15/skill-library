# Re2pect (Jordan Brand · 2014)

## Insight
Derek Jeter's retirement was not a sporting event — it was a cultural goodbye to a figure who transcended rivalry. Across 20 years of competition, every player, fan, and city had a specific relationship with Jeter that required acknowledgment. The campaign's job was to hold space for that complexity.

## Mechanic
A two-minute film set during Jeter's final season — showing opponent players, coaches, fans, and stadium workers giving him standing ovations and tips of the cap city by city. No narration. No product. The brand is present only as the cultural custodian of the moment. Title: "Re2pect" — using Jeter's uniform number 2 as punctuation.

## Why it worked
P16 (long-form drama) distilled to its purest form: let the subject carry the emotion, remove everything else. The brand's restraint — Jordan does not appear in the film beyond a brief logo — amplified the sincerity. The typography choice (Re2pect) was the only creative act needed; everything else was documentary reality. A genuinely unique cultural moment, irreproducible.

## Steal
When a public moment is already emotionally loaded, a brand's job is curation, not creation. The creative brief is: what is the minimum editorial frame that lets the moment breathe? Remove everything beyond that minimum.

# The MFCEO (K-Swiss · 2012)

## Insight
Athletic shoe advertising defaults to professional athletes and extreme performance. But K-Swiss, a mid-market brand without major athlete endorsements, could not compete on that battlefield. The insight: parody the alpha-male entrepreneurial persona that was becoming culturally prominent — the "hustle culture" CEO — and position K-Swiss as the shoe of that absurd archetype.

## Mechanic
A campaign featuring Kenny Powers character actor Danny McBride (Kenny from "Eastbound & Down") as K-Swiss's fictional CEO — the "MFCEO" — delivering profanity-filled motivational monologues about success, business, and winning while wearing K-Swiss shoes. Shot as a parody of corporate motivational content. Extended into social media, fake internal company memos, and in-character interviews.

## Why it worked
P11 (absurd as carrier): McBride's Kenny Powers persona is an already-established absurdist comic character; transplanting him into a corporate brand role creates immediate comedy through incongruity. P16: the campaign built a sustained character narrative across multiple media. For a brand without resources for real athlete endorsements, finding a way to own cultural commentary about sports culture was genuinely clever.

## Steal
When you cannot compete on the category's dominant axis (athlete endorsements, performance claims), find the cultural conversation happening around that axis and enter it sideways. Parody the adjacent culture rather than pretending to participate in it earnestly.

# 5-Man Job (KAYAK · 2020)

## Insight
Travel search should be simple, but people overcomplicate it by involving too many opinions. KAYAK's utility is speed and consolidation — it does the job better alone than through committee.

## Mechanic
An absurdist spot playing on heist-movie conventions, applying the "5-man job" archetype to travel planning — framing the search for the right flight as a multi-person operation, then undercutting it with the simplicity of KAYAK.

## Why it worked
The heist genre tropes are universally recognizable, and applying them to travel search amplifies the everyday anxiety of comparison shopping into something comic. The punchline — you don't need a crew — delivers the product benefit through contrast rather than explanation.

## Steal
Apply genre conventions from action or thriller films to mundane purchase decisions to expose the absurdity of how much energy people waste on them. The genre gap is the joke; the product is the resolution.

# Kenzo World (Kenzo · 2016)

## Insight
Luxury fragrance advertising is uniformly the same: beautiful people, slow movement, inexplicable scenarios, earnestness. The audience is sophisticated enough to recognise the category codes and feel imprisoned by them.

## Mechanic
Spike Jonze directs Margaret Qualley escaping a boring gala through a five-minute unbroken dance sequence of escalating physical extremity and absurdist joy. No fragrance shown until the final seconds. No romance. No conventional beauty signalling. Pure kinetic liberation.

## Why it worked
The film is genuinely worth watching independent of the brand. The invisible brand strategy (P07) earns trust; the extraordinary craft (P08) earns attention; the long form (P16) earns the emotion. The brand appears only when the release is earned. The anti-convention of the whole film signals what the brand is by showing what it is not.

## Steal
When your category is locked into a visual convention, commission a director known outside advertising. Give them the brief, not the execution. The result will look like their work — and nothing like the category.

# 11 Herbs and Spices (KFC · 2017)

## Insight
The internet's attention economy rewards brands that behave unexpectedly, not brands that broadcast. KFC's secret recipe — 11 herbs and spices — is its most famous mystery. Making that mystery visible through an impossibly tiny gesture was more powerful than any announcement.

## Mechanic
KFC's Twitter account followed exactly 11 people: the five Spice Girls and six men named Herb. A fan spotted this, tweeted about it, and the story went global. KFC did not announce or explain the stunt — they simply waited for discovery. The account had been configured this way for months before anyone noticed.

## Why it worked
P09 (user as co-author): the audience discovered the joke and became the news. P11 (absurd as carrier): the logic is internally perfect — 11 herbs, 11 follows, Herb + Spice Girls — and can be explained in one sentence, but only after you stare at it. Zero paid media. The entire campaign cost the creative idea and the patience to wait. The D&AD Yellow Pencil validated that the smallest possible act, executed with precision, beats the biggest possible media buy.

## Steal
Design for discovery, not for announcement. Hide a perfectly formed joke in plain sight and let the audience be the journalist. The moment of discovery — "wait, did they really do this?" — generates more earned media than any press release.
